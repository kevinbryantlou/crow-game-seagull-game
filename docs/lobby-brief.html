§0 Where this stands

Built, played, and revised once. This document was written before any geometry existed, as the argument and the numbers, so that the geometry could be checked against something. It has since been built, checked, played and corrected — the playtest round is §8, and it took out the ceiling, the chandelier and the front door. The claims that survived have been checked: smoke runs every level-design rule against it and shoot drives it in real WebGL, photographs eleven frames of it and measures six dusk samples. Both pass.

What the plan got wrong is in §7, and it is the interesting part — three of the four corrections were things no amount of writing would have found, because they are properties of a picture. The section is written as a record rather than tidied away, because that is what the last three briefs are for.

The premise came from the brief for this session: the roofline is the hotel from outside, so the block after it is the hotel from inside. That is the one piece of progression this game has never done — every block so far has been a new place, and this one is the same place through a different door. It is worth doing precisely once, and the last block is where it belongs.

The ladder, with a fourth rung $20 on the block · $25 in the park · $30 on the roofline · $35 here. One deck · two decks · four decks · three, stacked inside one room. Four equal dollar steps, one rung per block. It was built at $40 on an argument about the fiction and came down to $35 on a note about the run; §3 keeps both.
Copy rule for this block American English throughout. Fries, not chips. Bill, not note. Sidewalk, not pavement. Grab, not lift. Every string a player can read — task, toast, label, ending — gets checked against this before it ships, because the whole job of a line like The kid on the suitcase will trade for that is to send a specific person to a specific place, and a word they have to translate first does not do that.

§1 What a fourth block is for

Three blocks exist and each spends a different currency. The block spends nothing — it is the tutorial with a sunset on it. The park spends social pressure: three people round one cooler and no softer district to work instead. The roofline spends altitude: four decks, a nest twelve metres up, money priced by the climb.

A fourth block that spends more of any of those is a bigger version of a block that already exists. So the lobby spends the one thing none of them has touched: enclosure.

The one-room rule

Every block so far is open at the edges. You are caught at the cart, and you go and work the café row; you are caught at the picnic, and the pretzel wagon is forty metres east. The lobby is 44 m by 24 m with walls on all four sides, and every guard in it shares a room with every other guard. There is no district next door. There is no next door.

That is a genuinely nastier level, and left alone it would be a miserable one — a room where being seen has no answer is a room you lose in. So the lobby pairs it with the thing that makes it playable, and the pairing is the design:

The trade this block makes Laterally, there is nowhere to run. Vertically, nobody can follow. A human's floorY is authored and they never leave it, so the mezzanine at 4.4 m and the chandelier at 7.6 m are two rooms nobody in the cast can reach. The loop becomes: drop in, take one thing, climb out of the world. That is a new-feeling level made entirely out of verbs the player already has.

It also fixes the one thing that is slightly wrong with banking on every other block. The nest has always been in a corner — a memorial, a vent housing, a water tank — somewhere out of the way. Here the nest is in the chandelier over the middle of the room, so every single trip to it is a flight straight up through the centre of the lobby in front of everybody. The safest place on the block is also the most conspicuous, and that sentence is the whole level.

No new verbs Fly, take one thing, bank it, shove the weight off a bill, bait a guard with food, trade a shiny with a kid. All six are level 1's. The only new noun is the croissant, a fourth member of BAIT_KINDS — a pretzel that got in through the roof, same as the pigeons eating it.

§2 The plan

One room, read left to right: the bell desk and the stairs at the west end, the front desk across the back, the fountain and the kid dead centre under the chandelier, the bar and the lounge east. The mezzanine runs the full width along the back wall at 4.4 m, and the housekeeping cart is on it — so the safe deck is not free either.

The near wall is cut away, the way the roofline cuts away everything under its terrace. What is left at the section line is a 1.15 m glass screen on a brass rail and the sidewalk beyond it, which is the same street the roofline's forecourt stands on. The room stops somewhere without anything standing between the camera and the floor.

Exhibit A — the lobby in plan. The back wall is up; the camera is fixed below, looking up-screen at 38°. Roughly 21.8 px to the metre. The room is x −22…22, z −11.4…13 — the smallest block in the game, deliberately.

The three decks

  • 0 — the floor. Marble, a teal runner down the middle, and everybody in the cast except one.
  • 4.4 — the mezzanine. A 4 m gallery the full width of the back wall, reachable on foot by the grand stair and in one hop by a crow. The housekeeper works it and two gulls have got in and are standing on it.
  • 6.97 — the top of the lobby clock. A stone pillar north of the fountain with a brass lantern crown, and the nest in the crown. The moulded band at 3.3 m keeps the climb honest: 3.3 m, then 3.7 m, against a 9 m rule. It was a chandelier until the playtest took the ceiling away — §8.

Three decks after the roofline's four is not a step backwards. The roofline's decks are places — a forecourt, a terrace, a roof, each with its own economy. The lobby's are one place seen from three heights, which is why they can be stacked inside a room a third the size and still not feel like a smaller level.

$40, and then $35

It was built at $40. The argument was that $20 / $25 / $30 is three equal steps and the honest fourth term is $35, but the series was never the point — the last block is the inside of a building whose outside asked $30, and a hotel front desk is the only place in this game where a large amount of cash sitting in the open is not a contrivance. $35 in a room with a cash drawer in it would read as the level being coy.

That was an argument about the fiction. The playtest note was about the run — $40 is too much of an ask — and the run wins, which is exactly what this section said would happen if it ever came to it. It cost one line and the geometry did not care. Worth keeping the reasoning on both sides: the fiction argument is still true, it is just not worth a run that grinds.

The kid's ladder

tradeValues: [2.00, 3.00, 4.00, 5.00], a rung above the roofline's. She is sitting in the middle of an open marble floor, which is the easiest kid in the game to reach and the worst place in the game to be standing still. Four shinies exist — a room key fob, a paper clip, a foil wrapper, and a ring in the fountain — so the entire ladder pays $14.00. With $5.40 of free money that is $19.40 against a $35 goal, so trading can soften this block by rather more than half and can never finish it. That bound is smoke's to enforce, not mine to eyeball.

§4 The front desk

Every block has one set piece that the whole level is arranged around: the hot dog cart, the picnic cooler, the restaurant terrace. Here it is the front desk — $28.00 across twelve metres of counter, with two people behind it and a guest in front of it.

It is three problems in one place, and they are deliberately solved in different orders by different players:

  • The mail slot ($3.00) is a reach over the counter at the far end from the clerk. Cheap, and it teaches the counter's geometry for free.
  • The tip under the brass bell ($5.00) needs a shove first — this block's saltshaker, its paperback, its candle lantern. A shove is two beats long and it happens on the counter directly in front of the clerk.
  • The cash drawer ($20.00) is the concierge's, he stands at the open end of the desk where the camera can see him, and he does not move for anything except pigeons in his lobby.

Which is the croissant. Drop it on the floor more than 8 m from the desk and every pigeon in the atrium goes for it; the concierge leaves the drawer and goes to deal with the fact that there are birds in his lobby. Drop it on the mezzanine and nothing happens, because birds do not use stairs — the roofline teaches that lesson with a failure and this block is late enough to be allowed to assume it.

Why the concierge stands in front of his own desk The park learned this: its vendor stands at the open end of his wagon rather than behind it, because behind it is where the camera cannot see him, and level 1's newsagent spent a whole build reading as a head growing out of a shelf. A 1.15 m counter is low enough to keep the clerk legible, but the guard whose leaving is the puzzle has to be unmistakably visible both before and after he leaves. He stands in the open.

§5 The armchair problem

The kid is identified by her silhouette: she sits, and nothing else in the game sits. The roofline invented that and the park had to defend it — a park is mostly benches, so the picnic stands, the keeper walks, the jogger runs, and all four benches are empty.

A hotel lobby is worse than a park. A lobby is a room whose entire purpose is chairs.

So the lobby defends the silhouette twice over:

  • Every armchair in the lounge is empty. Four of them, arranged around a low table, all unoccupied. This is not even a stretch — an empty armchair in a hotel lobby is the most ordinary object in the world.
  • She does not sit on a chair. She sits on a hard-shell suitcase in the middle of an open marble floor, which is a shape nothing else in the room has: small, low, and nowhere near the furniture. A kid parked on the family luggage while the parents check in is the single most recognisable thing that happens in a hotel lobby, and it reads from the back of the frame.

She is also placed by the park's rule, not the roofline's: on the camera side of the fountain, so the water is behind her instead of in front. A small figure against teal is a small figure you can see; a small figure in front of a marble floor and a marble counter is a tan smudge. And nothing that is core loop goes behind the two occluders — the revolving door is at the far west end of the near wall and the palm is east of her.

The rest of the cast, and what each is for Concierge — the drawer, and the bait. Clerk — the bell and the mail slot, behind the counter. Bartender — the tray and the register. Bellhop — the level's clock: a full lap of the floor between the door and the desk, the way the park's keeper laps the park. Housekeeper — the mezzanine, so the safe deck costs something. A guest at the desk — owns nothing, notices nothing, stands exactly where you want to land. Someone crossing the lobby on their phone — a moving obstacle on the one route everybody uses. Eight, counting the kid; the park has seven.

§6 The sun, indoors

This was the part of the proposal most likely to be wrong. It was wrong, and the way it was wrong is the most useful thing in this document.

The entire light rig is a sunset. One directional key that swings and reddens, a hemisphere fill whose sky colour is the colour of shade, and a gradient sky sphere — all of it outdoors by construction. Rewriting it for one level was never on the table, so the lobby was given a glass lantern instead of a ceiling: a steel truss roof at 11.2 m glazed between the members, and a clerestory band from 6.6 m up on all four sides. The panes cast no shadow and the truss members do, so the afternoon key would come through as bars of light lying across the marble.

What that missed

This camera is above the roof It sits 38° up and about thirty metres back, so the sightline from anything on the floor leaves through the ceiling. A ceiling with structure in it is not a ceiling, it is a grille laid over the whole level — the pickups, the people and the crow. The audit caught five hidden pickups. Nothing would have caught the crow.

So the roof is sectioned away exactly like the near wall, and where the structure went is arithmetic rather than taste. A ray toward this camera rises 0.616 for every 0.714 it travels in +z, which means a thing standing on the floor crosses the ceiling plane 12.9 m further forward than it stands. The frontmost pickup on this block is at z = −8.2, so nothing on the floor leaves through the ceiling before z = +4.7, and nothing on the gallery before z = −3.2. That buys back a real ceiling:

  • The back seven metres are solid plaster, coffered, with downlights in it — over the gallery, which needed them more than anything else in the room did.
  • The rest is the lantern, whose trusses run across and never along. A beam parallel to z sits over a strip of floor six metres to its west and would hide everything on it; a beam parallel to x is over a strip 12.9 m behind it, which on this block is outside the room.
  • The chandelier hangs from the truss at z = 2, which is why that truss is at z = 2. The first build had it hanging off nothing, which a screenshot caught in one frame and no test would ever have.

And the bars of light come from somewhere better. The sun's azimuth swings from −118° to −138° across a session — behind the block and to the west, all day — so the clerestory mullions on the west and back walls cast them, and those are the two walls that can never occlude anything. The east wall has no mullions at all, for the same reason its solid half stops at 6.6 m: it is the one wall the camera looks through.

Starting at t = 0.55

The block starts at 0, the park at 0.20, the roofline at 0.42. The lobby starts at 0.55, so RULES.lampsOnAt (0.72) arrives at 3 m 01 s of the eight-minute day, against the roofline's 4 m 08 s and the block's 5 m 46 s. This is the first block where most players will finish in artificial light, which is the honest reading of walking into a hotel lobby at six in the evening — and it makes the chandelier catching the level's event, the way the street lights catching are the block's.

The rule that did not get bent duskMedianFloor and duskShadowFloor were not lowered to make this block pass. It failed them twice and the materials changed both times. Shipped: median 48 / 61 against a floor of 48, 5th percentile 35 / 42 against a floor of 24, blue over red by 18 and 34 at t = 0.98.

§7 What the harnesses caught

Four corrections, in the order they were found. Three of them are properties of a picture and one is a property of a number, which is roughly the ratio this project always gets.

1. The ceiling was a grille over the level

Five pickups hidden, reported by the audit's sightline ray; the cause, and the fix, are §6. The lesson generalises past this block: a camera above a roof means the roof is between it and everything. An interior in this engine is not an outdoor block with a lid on it.

2. The chandelier photographed as a wedding cake

Two thick gold discs with a cone between them. Legible in the source, and from a camera looking down at 38° it read as tiered woodwork — nothing about it said light fitting. What says it from above is a wheel: a thin pale pan, a brass rim, spokes to a hub, candles standing on the rim and drops hanging off the edge. This is the roofline's kid all over again — the fix for "you cannot tell what that is" is the silhouette, not the shade.

3. The runner, twice

It was specified as a sage-teal so the lobby would carry the park's colour chord indoors. It photographed as grass: a long green band running off the edge of the frame, on the one block in this game trying not to read as outdoors. Corrected to a dusty blue — and at dusk that rendered as a navy band across a third of the lower frame and pinned the 5th percentile at 21 against a floor of 24.

Two lounge floor lamps and two wall sconces moved that number by nothing at all, because a pool of light on the floor cannot make a dark material bright. The runner is 34 points of luminance below the park's lawn, and the lawn is the palest thing the park could get away with. Repainting it pale blue-grey moved the 5th percentile from 21 to 35 in one edit. The lamps stayed, because the lounge and the west end genuinely had no light of their own — but they were the wrong first move, exactly as the roofline's brief says.

The rule, stated for the fourth time Find the biggest dark thing before adding a lamp — and a big surface is a light-rig decision before it is a colour one.

4. A test that was wrong about the level

The check written for the chandelier — "the crown is landable from every heading" — flew a crow at it from five metres out and reported all eight headings as unreachable. The crown was fine. A crow crossing a three-metre disc at flight speed has 0.35 s to fall 1.4 m, so it sailed over every time. The check now drops a crow onto eight points round the rim with no input at all, which asserts the thing actually worth asserting: the whole disc is a floor, edges included, because banking is the only reason anybody goes up there and you arrive carrying.

And what the plan got right

The economy needed no tuning: $57.05 on the block, $5.40 unguarded and dry, $50.40 guarded, $19.40 reachable without stealing against a $40 goal. The decks needed none: the tallest unbroken climb to this nest measures 1.7 m. Nothing shoves the crow more than 0.55 m in a frame despite the mezzanine, the stair landings, four counters and two chandelier tiers all being colliders with air underneath them. The pass-through in the middle of the front desk was drawn on the first attempt because the park had already taught that a guard in a corridor is a guard who cannot chase, and the chase probe that starts behind the desk passes because of it.

§8 What the playtest took out

Three notes, and between them they removed the ceiling, the chandelier and the front door. All three are the same note: this camera sits above the room, so everything near the top of it is between the player and the floor. §6 and §7 had already learned half of that from an automated check. The half a check cannot see is that "in the way" includes the crow, the people, and the light — not only pickups.

The lantern went

The glass panes tinted the whole frame, the truss over the middle laid a bar across it, and a row of clerestory mullions on the west wall hung over the staircase. That last one was a plain bug and a good example of a kind: the mullion builder took the wall's length and thickness in the wrong order on the two short walls, so every "mullion" on the west wall was a fourteen-metre slab floating at 6.6 m. Seven of them. Every automated check this block has looks at the floor, so nothing caught it and one screenshot did.

What is left over the room is nothing at all: a plastered soffit over the back seven metres where the gallery is, a cornice capping each wall head, and open air over everywhere anybody plays.

Which cost the chandelier

A hanging fitting needs something overhead to hang from, and with the ceiling gone every candidate was a bar across the camera. A chain rising into a ceiling that is not drawn is the same problem in disguise — it either ends in mid-air or leaves the frame, and both read as unfinished.

So the nest sits on something that stands on the floor: a lobby clock, five metres north of the fountain on the room's centre line. It keeps everything the chandelier was carrying — the crown is the same brass corona moved down and given a pedestal, so the block still has one lit thing owning the middle of the frame at dusk; the cornice is 3.2 m across against a 1.5 m nest; and banking is still a flight up through the middle of the lobby in front of everybody, which is the sentence this level is built on.

It is beside the fountain rather than in it, and that is not taste. The audit forbids building inside the water, and the escape test drops a crow at the exact centre of the basin and requires it to get out — which a stem standing there would prevent. "Ornamental fountain centrepiece" is the obvious reading of the note and it is a trap twice over.

And the front door

A crow could get outside and strand itself on the sidewalk. The frontage was a 1.2 m glass screen with the invisible rest of the wall above it on the same footprint, so a crow that landed on the screen was standing inside the wall above and got resolved out through the nearer face — which on the near wall of a room means the street. That is the crow's own lateral-collision rule doing exactly what it is supposed to. One collider now, floor to roof, perch: false: nothing to stand on, so nothing to be pushed off.

The revolving door went with it, after two attempts. As a glazed drum it photographed as a teal barrel with an orange lid; rebuilt as an open brass frame it photographed as a café table with a parasol. The diagnosis is not about modelling — a revolving door is a hole in a wall, and this block no longer has a near wall. What replaced it is the part that was doing the work: a mat, a rope line and a lamp standard, none of which is taller than a person.

The rule this block adds The audit asks "can the camera see this pickup". A playtest asks "is this in the way", and those are different questions. Anything above about four metres in an interior has to answer the second one before it is allowed to exist.

§9 What changes outside this level

Deliberately almost nothing. The progression system is two fields and a function, and it was built for this.

  • world/lobby.js — new. Named for the place, like park.js; slot numbers are a registry question, not a filename one.
  • world/levels.js — a fourth descriptor, and level 3's next goes from null to 4. That one edit is the entire unlock change: the ending screen grows a brass The lobby → button, the Levels screen grows a fourth chip, and save.js opens the door — all of it off next, which is why the ladder is defined in exactly one place.
  • scripts/shoot.mjs — the lobby's frames are 50–59, not 40–49: 40 was already the touch pause control, and two sections writing one filename is a screenshot that quietly stops being the thing it is named after.
  • render/palette.js — two entries, rug and rugMid, plus the matching swatches in docs/style-guide.html. The doc is the spec; if they disagree the doc is wrong, so they get changed together.
  • world/pickups.js — the croissant mesh and label, and croissant added to BAIT_KINDS. The audit reads that set rather than naming kinds, so nothing else has to notice.
  • scripts/shoot.mjs — a level 4 section: the block photographed at both ends of the day, the dusk measurement, and the navigation test extended to the fourth chip.

What is not being changed: the roofline's ending copy. It closes on a kid holding out a marble, it does not name where you go next, and the button under it already does. And no level file gets renamed.

The ending

Losing headline: Check Out.

Winning: the game's last ending, so it closes the arc rather than pointing anywhere. You come back twenty-five feet over a marble floor, in a hotel you are not a guest of, with a nest full of money at your elbow and every face in the lobby turned up at you — except the kid's, who is still holding a room key out at knee height for a bird that is not there any more.

§11 Questions, open and closed

Closed

  • Interior or another exterior? Interior. It is the one progression this game has not made, and the roof of this building was the last block.
  • How does the sun get in? A glass lantern roof and a clerestory. No change to the light rig.
  • Where is the nest? The chandelier, over the middle of the room. It is the level's thesis in one object.
  • Does the mezzanine make people follow you up? No. Humans never leave their deck, and that is the deal this block is built on.

Open — decide by measuring

  • $40 or $35. Closed: $35, on a playtest note. §3 keeps both arguments.
  • Two gulls on the mezzanine, or none. They are there so the safe deck is not free. Untouched by the first playtest, which spent itself on the ceiling. If they make the only route to the nest read as punishing, they come out and the housekeeper carries it alone.
  • Whether the clock is a landmark or an obstacle. It is seven metres of stone on the room's centre line, which is the trade the chandelier did not make. One playtest has seen it; the frames say it reads, a run has not.
  • Whether the runner survives both ends of the light ramp. Closed by measurement, twice — see §7.
  • Whether a room this size wants eight people in it. The park's seven are spread over 60 m; these eight are in 44. If it reads as crowded rather than watched, the phone-crosser is the first one out.
