§1 What changed, and what's left to decide

The first version of this brief was built on canyons — tall container rows making corridors you walk down blind. You vetoed it: fit the level to the engine, don't occlude a meaningful amount of the navigable map. That was right, and the replacement is better, because it turns out the engine already hands you the mechanic.

The whole idea, in one line A container is 2.4 m tall. That hides 2.78 m of deck from the camera and hides everything from a guard. So the player sees the whole board and the crew see almost nothing — and since a guard can only reach 1.9 m above their own feet, standing on any box is safe.

Three of the five decisions in the last version have evaporated. Two remain.

1
Is "you see everything, they see nothing" the level? It inverts the master outline, which wanted to take information away from the player. This gives the player more information than any block so far and takes it from the guards instead. Everything below assumes it.
2
$45 Next rung of the $5 ladder. My recommendation is hold it: the bank is the shortest trip in the game and the deck is fast to cross. First thing to cut to $40 if a playtest reads slow. One line.

Settled: the ship is at sea with an empty backdrop; no ship roll; the nest is on a mast amidships; the guard line-of-sight fix is already shipped and live, so this level is being designed against behaviour that exists rather than behaviour I am asking for. That is new, and it is why this version needs no engine changes at all.

§2 The two numbers it's built on

Both are measured off shipped code, not chosen.

A camera 28 m up and an eye 1.6 m up see completely different worlds

The camera is fixed at 38° down and never tilts, so a solid hides 1.16× its own height of floor behind it. A 2.4 m container therefore costs the player a 2.78 m shadow — about the size of the shadow a litter bin or a newsstand already casts on level 1.

A guard's eye is at 1.60 m. A 2.4 m box is 0.8 m over their head, so it hides everything behind it, all the way to the horizon.

The gap between those two is the level. It costs nothing to build — it is a consequence of the camera this game has always had.

A guard can only reach 1.9 m above their own feet

Human.update gives up on catching the crow once it is 1.9 m above the guard's own floor, and the actual shoo needs 1.6 m. A container top is 2.4 m — or 3.0 m when the box stands on a hatch cover. So one hop up is always safe, everywhere on the ship, and it is safe by 0.5 m rather than by a hair.

This is already true on every block — any 2 m perch does it. No block has ever made it the structure. Here the deck is made of the safe thing.

And a check on the whole idea: how hidden is hidden?

Your note was that a meaningful amount of the navigable map must not be occluded. I did not have a number for what "meaningful" is, so I measured the shipped blocks — ground-floor walkable deck, hidden from the fixed camera, invisible map-bounds excluded:

Which turns your note into a rule the build can be held to No more than 35% of the deck hidden — no worse than level 1 — and no hidden pocket on a route or under a pickup. The second half is the one that matters, and rule 6 already enforces it for pickups. The first half I will measure and report, with the same script that produced the table above.

§3 The loop

The deck — 0 and 0.6

  • Open steel. You can walk anywhere, fast, no corners.
  • All the money is down here.
  • Everyone can reach you.
  • But they can only see as far as the next box.

The boxes — 2.4 and 3.0

  • Out of arm's reach. Nobody can follow.
  • Every one is in frame, so you always see your bird.
  • Islands — crossing between them costs a wingbeat.
  • Nothing to take up here. It is a place to wait, not to work.

So the run is: watch, drop, grab, hop back up. You have perfect information — every guard, every heading, every cone — and the thing you want is always on the floor with them. The block is a timing puzzle you can actually solve, rather than a memory test.

What makes it more than a waiting game is that the guards are also half blind. Since the sight fix shipped, a crewman cannot see through his own cargo. So you are not just timing a cone sweeping past — you are working an approach where he never gets a clear line at all, and the boxes that hide you from him cost you almost nothing in return.

Why this is not the roofline again The roofline spends altitude: money is priced by the climb, and getting up is the work. Here the climb is one flap and it is free. Height is not a reward, it is a pause button — and the cost of pressing it is that you are not carrying anything while you do. Every other block rewards being up. This one just makes it safe.

The hatch covers are the quiet trick. A real ship's deck is mostly hatch cover, and at 0.6 m they sit below the 0.85 m threshold at which a solid becomes cover — so they break the floor into levels, give the deck scale and texture, and hide 0.70 m of ground from the camera and nothing at all from a guard. Free geography.

The two-high stacks are the only tall cargo, and they sit against the port rail specifically because a 4.8 m box throws a 5.6 m shadow — put there, it lands on the bulwark and the sea rather than on anywhere you play. Silhouette variety at no cost. Same argument puts the house at the west end: the funnel at 9.4 m throws 10.9 m, and from the west end that goes off the map.

Nothing forms a corridor. Boxes stand in ones and twos with open steel between them, which is also what a part-loaded ship actually looks like.

§5 The nest, the water, the money

The nest

A mast amidships with a platform at 7.0 m. The crow's nest is a crow's nest.

It sits in the cross lane between the two hatch covers, so it is central and every bank is short — the shortest in the game. Three rules it satisfies: the platform is 3.2 m across against a 1.5 m nest (2.13×, needs 2); the mast is a bare pole with no gallery under the platform, so there is no lid to be trapped beneath; and the masthead light hangs on an arm cantilevered from below the platform, because anything rooted above it sits in the column the audit checks — the mistake the lobby clock made.

Drawn so the three answers are visible.

The water

A crew pool on the aft deck, 9 × 7 m. Ships this size have one; a real one is a quarter the size, and the size here is set by the escape harness — a 4 × 4 pool yields one grid cell and would report a clean pass having checked almost nothing. It needs 16.6 × 14.6 m of clear deck around it, because the approach runs sit 3.2 m out and the collider is the basin, 0.6 m wider than the waterline on every side. The aft deck is 18 × 19.6, so it fits.

The sea is out of bounds — the hull is 2.6 m of vertical steel, and a crow in the water alongside is a lobster pot, which this project has shipped three times. Which leaves the joke: surrounded by an entire ocean, the only water it can swim in is a steel box on the back deck.

The mate is the block's counterweight and the reason it is not solved. Everyone else is at 1.6 m with cargo in the way; he is at 6.6 m with nothing in the way at all, because the deck is deliberately low. So the west end — the pool, the kid, the mess room ten — is the one part of the ship where the boxes do not help you, and that is where a third of the money is.

They are working because that is what a deck gang does on passage: tightening lashings, chipping, painting. No cargo work, no crane.

Silhouette

The kid is legible because she sits and almost nothing else in the game does. A ship's deck is full of things that read as seats — bollards, bitts, coamings, rope coils — so this block has to work at it. Nobody else sits.

Gulls, and they have to be two groups

Eight gulls. They are also the level-5 callback now that there is no landmark: a ship on passage tows gulls for days, so these are the wharf's gulls and they followed you out.

A bird only comes to food within 2.2 m of its own floor, so a gull on a 2.4 m box is already out of range of a bacon roll on the deck, by 20 cm. Four go low — rails, deck, hatch tops — and those are the mob. Four go high on the boxes and will never come to food. Not one flock.

No pigeons. There are none at sea. Worth confirming on day one that the set piece fires without them, because no block has ever shipped without pigeons.

§7 How it looks

The deck is the biggest surface in the game

Roughly 60 × 21 m of continuous paint — bigger than the park's lawn, twice the lobby's floor. Both took three attempts, and both failed the same way: a colour picked at one end of the light ramp and never checked at the other.

A real ship's deck is red oxide or deck green, and both are ruled out by arithmetic this repo has already paid for. Red oxide is saturated, so it has two low channels and goes near-black in shade — that killed the wharf's first container palette, and those were accents, not 1200 m² of ground. Deck green goes olive under the warm afternoon key, and olive on a floor is turf.

Indicative, not measured. The build checks the real frame at t=0 and t=0.98 before anything is painted.

Cool steel grey, with the hatch covers a half-step darker so the deck is not one flat field, and painted non-slip strips on the walkways as decals.

The containers carry all the colour

PAL.container already holds four hues that were backed off from saturated when the wharf's first palette failed dusk. That work is done. With only eleven boxes the wallpaper risk from the canyon version is gone — these read as objects, not as a wall.

Making it read as a ship

You never see the horizon, the hull, or both ends at once, so the ship cannot be established by silhouette. It has to be established by edges: two bulwark rails running the full 60 m with sea below and beyond, water moving on both sides at once, the house and funnel at one end only, and lashing rods and turnbuckles at the box feet — the one detail that could not be anything else. A static bow wave and wake say she is moving. No roll.

Dusk

dayStart: 0.30, so the deck lights catch at 4m48s of an eight-minute day. Deliberately not the latest in the set: at t=0.58 the key light multiplies blue by 0.35, which is what turned the wharf's harbour brown. This block has water on two sides and a grey deck between them, so it has more to lose from a late start than any block yet.

Few lights, small, bright — the lobby's lesson, where thirty pools cost 38 ms a frame. Twelve pools, none over 5 m: the house front, the mast, the walkway ends, the pool coping, and one on each hatch cover.

This should be the cheapest block in the game. Eleven containers is twenty-two meshes and four materials. No trees, no planting, no café furniture, no crowd, a six-mesh backdrop, and a deck that is mostly flat paint.

What the tests will and won't catch

  • No pickup hidden from the camera — the rule that keeps the money legible, and with a sparse deck it should pass easily rather than by a whisker.
  • Everything resting on a deck that exists — box tops at 2.4 and 3.0 and hatch covers at 0.6 make it easy to write a coordinate and forget the tier.
  • The coplanar-face rule will not catch stacked containers (different tops), so do not expect it to. What it will catch is a bulwark rail cap drawn flush with the bulwark top, and a container sitting exactly flush on a hatch cover. Watch those.
  • Tag only the starboard bulwark edge-kerb — the rule takes the first match, and this is the first block with a kerb-like collider at both edges.
  • Chase probes: an open deck is the easiest pathing case in the game, so the lattice worry from the canyon version is gone. Probes still go round the house, the pool and each hatch cover.

Two checks worth adding: print the hidden-ground fraction every run so the 35% budget is enforced rather than claimed, and assert a guard cannot see across the deck through a container — the mechanic the whole block rests on, which is currently an inference from a shipped fix rather than a statement about this level.

Wiring

  • Not from up on the boxes — the wrong-deck failure, and on this block the wrong answer is three metres straight up, which is exactly where a player will try it, because that is where they have been standing all game.

Plain words for places. No bulwark, no fo'c'sle, no coaming in anything a player reads.

The ending

Being at sea is worth two endings. Every loss so far ends with the level still there and nobody watching — the fountain is full of coins and you have all night. That needs somewhere you could come back to. Out here there is nowhere to go, and it lands funnier rather than bleaker: you have four days. And the win gets a joke the set has been setting up for six blocks: forty-five dollars, earned in the one place on earth where money buys nothing.

Title: Night<span>Watch</span>.

Won: "The last note goes into the nest and every deck light on the ship comes on at once, bow to stern, because somebody on the bridge threw one switch.

You come back seven metres over a container deck, on a steel platform three metres across with a rail round it and nothing above it but sky. There is no land in any direction and there will not be for four days, which makes forty-five dollars the most useless fortune anybody ever earned. Down by the rail a kid is still holding a brass tally out at arm's length for a bird that is not there any more."

Lost: "You got to $X. The deck lights are on, the watch has changed, and the sea has gone the colour of a bruise all the way out to an edge you cannot see.

Still a crow. But nothing on this ship is going anywhere for four days, and neither is the twenty under the mug on number three hatch."

The premise survives it: the curse is collect the sum, not spend it. A crow banking twenty dollars was never going to buy anything on any block, so "the money is useless out here" is a joke in the ending and not a hole in the loop.

§10 Open questions

  1. Is one hop up too safe? The honest risk in this design is the opposite of the last one: if standing on a box is always safe and always available, the block may have no teeth. The mate is the intended answer, and the money being all on the floor is the other. If a playtest reads as trivial, the first lever is fewer boxes, not taller ones.
  2. Does an empty backdrop read as finished? You have said yes given enough interesting things across the ship, so the deck has to earn it — the wake, the lashing gear, the ragged load, the house.
  3. Does the bait fire with no pigeons and four low gulls? Day one, before any geometry is nice.
  4. Does the guard sight fix feel right at this density? This is the first block designed around it rather than corrected by it, and your playtest of levels 1–5 is the first real evidence either way.
