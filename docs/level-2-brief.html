§1 What level 1 never used

The block is a good level and it is flat. Three districts run left to right, money density falls as you go, guard density rises, and the whole thing happens between y = 0 and y = 5.35. The crow has wings, a stamina bar, a glide, and a list of authored perches, and almost none of it is load-bearing: you can play a competent 2m07s run and never climb higher than the memorial you bank on.

So the second block does not add a verb. It takes the one system the first block installed and never spent — altitude — and makes it the price of everything.

The constraint, stated up front No new player mechanics. Fly, take one thing, bank it, shove the weight off the bill, bait a guard with food, trade a shiny with a kid. Every one of those is level 1's, taught on level 1, and charged for differently here. The only new entity is a gull, and §6 argues for it.

§2 The value ladder is the altitude ladder

On the block, a penny cost you a short walk and a ten cost you a plan. The denominations did the pricing and the geography was nearly free.

Up here the geography does the pricing. The nest is on a water tank twelve metres above the yard. A penny in the yard is worth one cent and costs a twelve-metre climb to bank, which is a bad trade and is supposed to be a bad trade. Nobody says so. There is no tooltip and no tutorial beat. The player works it out the first time they haul a nickel up four decks and watch the counter move by five cents.

That is the entire design. Everything below is consequence.

Exhibit A — the elevation. Money rises with the floor; so does the cost of banking it.

Why it is a staircase and not a cliff

A full stamina bar is 1.0 / 0.42 = 2.38 seconds of flapping at up to 6.4 m/s, so the theoretical ceiling is about fifteen metres. Sizing the level for that would be sizing it for a player who arrives at the bottom of every climb with a full bar — which is exactly the player who does not exist, because you arrive at a climb having just flown away from somebody.

So the tallest single hop on the block is 5.4 m, and even that one is broken up further: the fire escape has landings at 2.0, 3.65 and 5.25, the van roof is at 2.55, the loading dock at 1.15. Climbing is a rhythm of short hops and rests, and stamina regenerates while grounded — so a perch is the first thing on this block that is genuinely functional rather than scenery.

§3 The money

$56.70 on the block against a $40 goal. Of that, $7.05 is unguarded and dry — and almost all of it is coins in a yard twelve metres under the nest. The endgame is the terrace, in every possible order of play, and no difficulty tuning produces that: the layout does.

The twenty is new. Level 1's top prize was a ten against a twenty; this is a twenty against a forty, which is the same ratio and a bigger number, and it means the folded note in the maître d's bill-fold is worth half the level on its own.

§4 The four decks

The yard (y = 0)

Bins, crates, a delivery van, a loading dock, a drain. It is the bottom of a light well and the cheapest place on the block. Two things make it worth entering at all: the chef's tin by the kitchen door, and the fact that it is the only deck the chips can be wasted on.

The fire escape (2.0 / 3.65 / 5.25)

The authored route up, and the only part of the level that rewards not flying. The kid who trades shinies sits on the first landing — deliberately halfway, so a trade is always on the way to somewhere rather than a detour to the bottom.

The terrace (5.4)

Four fifths of the money, two guards, six gulls, five tables and a plunge pool. It is also the only deck with a parapet, which is a wall to a walker and a perch to a crow — and it has a deliberate gap in it at the head of the fire escape, because a 0.7 m lip is over the crow's 0.34 m scramble and without the gap every stair climb would end in one pointless extra flap.

Chimney Row (9.2), and the tank (12.35)

The crow's own territory: pots, an aerial, a pigeon loft, a window cleaner on his break, and the water tank with the nest on its lid. The lid is round and its collider is the square inside it, so the crow can never stand on a corner with nothing underneath — and at 3.35 m against a 1.5 m twig ring it clears the nest-platform rule with room to spare, which matters more here than on the memorial because you arrive at this one out of breath.

§6 The gulls, and why they are allowed

A roof has no cover on it. On the block you could put a plane tree between yourself and the waiter, or take the awning, or drop behind the fountain. On a fifty-metre terrace there is nothing to put anywhere, and a level whose whole pitch is "the money is up here" cannot also be a level where being up here is free.

So the danger is authored as birds. Six gulls hold fixed pitches — two on the parapet either side of the maître d's stand, one by the pool, two on the roof. Land inside one's patch and it shrieks; every person within eleven metres turns to look and gets a shove of suspicion. Fly over it and nothing happens, because a gull does not care about a bird two metres above its head and because punishing flight would punish the one thing the level is trying to teach.

They are markers that say not here. Unlike a guard they cannot be outrun — only avoided, or moved.

What this actually cost Gull extends Pigeon. A different mesh, a smaller wander range, and eleven lines that make a noise and hand the game a callback. The alarm runs through the same lookAt / _suspicion plumbing a caw already used — which is the point: a gull is the crow's own trick, used against it.

The joke it settles

This project exists because of an Instagram "would you rather" with two options: turned into a crow until you collect $20, or turned into a seagull until you eat 40 chips. The crow won, on the argument that $20 is a value ladder and 40 chips is 40 identical pickups. The seagulls got in anyway. So did the chips.

§7 The set piece

Level 1's marquee puzzle is a hot dog: drop it away from the cart, the pigeons mob it, the vendor leaves, and there are twelve seconds of unguarded ten. This block runs the same machinery and gets two effects out of one action.

There is a paper cone of chips on the fourth table. Drop it on the terrace, eight metres or more from the maître d's stand, and:

  • every gull on the terrace leaves its pitch for the chips — so an entire approach to the bill-fold goes quiet;
  • the maître d' goes to deal with them — so the thing at the end of that approach is unguarded.

Drop it in the yard instead and the yard's pigeons get a meal and the terrace carries on exactly as it was. That failure is the level's one deliberate teaching beat, and it is taught by failing rather than by a toast that arrives before you have done anything: birds do not use stairs. It is the cheapest possible way to tell a player that this block has floors.

And the weight

A candle lantern sits on the check on the third table. SHOVE it and the five comes loose. It is the saltshaker, exactly — the verb is taught on the block for free and charged for here, on a table beside a man who is paid to notice.

§8 What a second floor cost the engine

Almost everything in the game quietly assumed y = 0. Four generalisations were needed, all small, all of which leave level 1 bit-for-bit identical because it passes 0 everywhere.

  • Walkers have a floor. blocksWalker and friends take the deck the walker is standing on. Without it, a maître d' on a roof terrace is permanently inside the 5.4 m solid he is standing on and cannot take a step in any direction.
  • People and birds have a floorY. Reach, sightlines and rendering all measure from it. A person also sees height at a discount, so roof staff do not spend the session shooing at a crow five metres below their feet they could not reach if they wanted to.
  • Light pools have a y. A lamp on a terrace has to put its light on the terrace, not on the yard six metres below it.
  • Water has a floor as well as a surface. See below.

Two real bugs, found by building on top of things

Both are the kind that could not exist on a flat block and are invisible in review on a tall one.

  • The pool was a lobster pot again. The crow's scramble test read c.bottom <= 0.01 — "is this thing standing on the ground?" — which is the right question asked the wrong way. The plunge pool's rim stands on a terrace, so its bottom is 5.4, so a crow floating in it could not climb out at any heading at all. It now measures from the crow's own feet, which says the same thing on every floor. The fountain's basin was a trap once before for a completely different reason; this is the second time and the last, because the rule is now about a relationship rather than about zero.
  • You could swim in a pool five metres over your head. inWater was "inside the ring and below the surface", which is true of the entire column of air underneath a raised pool. Bounded below as well as above, and asserted from the yard.

And one that was already there

Pathing ran out of steering. stepAround deflected a blocked step by up to 112°, which is enough to get round a fountain and not enough to get round a seven-metre van: as a walker slides along a wide face away from its target, the direction to the target keeps rotating until every deflection under 112° still points into the wall. The committed side then has nothing free, the other side does, and the walker turns round — the old shuffle, arrived at from the opposite direction. Two wider angles were added, and a committed side is now held until two and a half metres of the road ahead are clear rather than until one step is.

§9 Three new rules

The contract moved out of world/level.js and into world/rules.js, because a rule that lives inside the level it constrains is not a contract, it is a comment. Every rule now runs against both blocks. Three are new, and all three are what a level with floors needs.

#RuleWhy it exists
9No climb longer than maxUnbrokenClimb = 9 m with nothing to land on 60% of what a full bar buys, so it clears on a bar that is two thirds empty. This is why the roofline is a staircase.

12There is somewhere legal to drop the bait The set piece has a deck requirement and a distance requirement. If they ever conflict, the level's marquee puzzle is quietly unsolvable.

§10 The light, and what it caught

This block starts at dayStart = 0.42 and gets the back 58% of the sunset ramp. Same eight minutes, less daylight in them: the lamps catch at 4m08s here against 5m46s on the block, so dusk arrives while you are still working rather than as a consolation for being slow.

That meant time-of-day is no longer the same number as progress through the run, which is exactly the class of arithmetic that has bitten this project twice. The HUD's sun dial is now handed elapsed seconds separately, and the lamp-timing assertion converts the trigger on to each level's own clock before comparing it to the rank ladder — in both directions, as the existing note demands.

The navigability floor did its job

The block must stay playable after dark: median luminance ≥ 48, 5th percentile ≥ 24, measured off the rendered frame with the HUD hidden. Level 2 failed it badly on the first measurement — 5th percentile of 6 — and the fix was not more light. It was four materials.

  • The hotel wall was terracotta. Fifty metres of it, facing away from a sun that comes from behind the block: it filled a third of the frame, was most of the darkest 5%, and dragged the whole dusk average red at the hour the palette says shade goes violet. Both failures, one material.
  • The yard was one step darker than the plaza — "wet asphalt, the bottom of a light well". It is the largest surface in frame from every camera position on the level, and the fiction was not worth the median.
  • The van was navy. A seven-metre navy box is a lot of dark.
  • Six service windows were unlit teal, and measured darker in daylight than the shadow they sat in — which is also just wrong. Glass seen from outside on a sunny afternoon is a mirror of the sky, not a hole.

Shipped figures, over six samples from two camera positions across the session: median 51–127, 5th percentile 27–41, and blue beats red in the frame average at dusk. The floors were not touched — lowering a shared threshold to make a new level pass is the failure mode this repo has a whole note about.

One level is built at a time, so nothing above is additive at runtime. The whole cost of a second block is what it adds to the bundle: 164.6 kB gzipped, against roughly 155 before — about 9 kB, nearly all of it geometry described in source. There is still no asset pipeline, nothing to download, and no new draw-call class: the gulls are pigeons, the pools are the same additive quads, and the terrace tables are the café tables from world/kit.js.

Where the shared parts live now world/kit.js holds anything a second block would otherwise copy-paste — tables, benches, lamps, bins, planters, the skyline, the nest, and the pool. A café table is kit. A war memorial with a nest on it is not. Level 1 was rebuilt on the kit first and re-verified before level 2 was written, so the refactor and the new level never had to be debugged at the same time.

§12 Deliberately not here

  • How you get from one block to the other. Selection is ?level=2 and main.js is the only thing that reads it. That is a seam, not a join, and it is left open on purpose.
  • Carrying anything between levels. No money, no rank, no state. Each block is a session.
  • A story that commits. The endings are written to stand alone. Level 1's win turns you back into a person on top of a war memorial; level 2's turns you back on top of a water tank, in front of a window cleaner who has questions. Neither depends on the other having happened.
  • New verbs. Still none. If a third block needs one, it should be because a third block's pacing demands it — not because the second one set a precedent.
