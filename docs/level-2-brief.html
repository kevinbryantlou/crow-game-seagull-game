§0 Where this stands

Built, played, and revised once against playtest notes. The headline note was that it is too big a scale leap to follow the block directly — so it is parked as a later level, three or four, once the blocks between exist. Everything else on this page assumes that.

The one number that moved because of it is the goal: $30, above the block's $20 and below the $40 it was first built at. A step on the ladder rather than a doubling.

What the revision changed Two rounds of it. First: the fountain came down off the roof into a forecourt garden, the kid moved off the fire escape, the pigeon loft went entirely, the terrace got doors, and two real bugs got fixed. Second: the block is fourteen metres wider, the fire escape is gone and Juliet balconies do its job, and the kid is sitting on the parapet — because standing on a terrace among a maître d', a busser and two diners, she was one more figure in a crowd and a playtester could not tell who to hand things to.

§1 What the block never used

Level 1 is a good level and it is flat. Three districts run left to right, money density falls as you go, guard density rises, and the whole thing happens between y = 0 and y = 5.35. The crow has wings, a stamina bar, a glide and a list of authored perches, and almost none of it is load-bearing: you can play a competent 2m07s run and never climb higher than the memorial you bank on.

So this block does not add a verb. It takes the one system the first block installed and never spent — altitude — and makes it the price of everything.

The constraint, stated up front No new player mechanics. Fly, take one thing, bank it, shove the weight off the bill, bait a guard with food, trade a shiny with a kid. Every one of those is level 1's, taught on level 1, and charged for differently here. The only new entity is a gull, and §7 argues for it.

§2 The value ladder is the altitude ladder

On the block, a penny cost you a short walk and a ten cost you a plan. The denominations did the pricing and the geography was nearly free.

Here the geography does the pricing. The nest is on a water tank twelve metres above the forecourt. A penny by the fountain is worth one cent and costs a twelve-metre climb to bank, which is a bad trade and is supposed to be a bad trade. Nobody says so. There is no tooltip and no tutorial beat. The player works it out the first time they haul a nickel up four decks and watch the counter move by five cents.

That is the entire design. Everything below is consequence.

Exhibit A — the elevation. Money rises with the floor; so does the cost of banking it.

Why it is a staircase and not a cliff

A full stamina bar is 1.0 / 0.42 = 2.38 seconds of flapping at up to 6.4 m/s, so the theoretical ceiling is about fifteen metres. Sizing the level for that would be sizing it for a player who arrives at the bottom of every climb with a full bar — which is exactly the player who does not exist, because you arrive at a climb having just flown away from somebody.

So the tallest single hop is 3.8 m, and every deck has something under it: seven balconies at 3.2 strung along sixty metres of frontage, the entrance canopy at 3.42, the van roof at 2.55, the loading dock at 1.15. Climbing is a rhythm of short hops and rests, and stamina regenerates while grounded — so a perch is the first thing in this game that is functional rather than scenery.

§3 The forecourt, and why it is not a yard

The ground floor was a service yard: bins, crates, a van, a loading dock, and nothing a person would walk into on purpose. It measured fine and it read as nowhere.

It is now the front of a hotel: a fountain, four planted beds, a striped entrance canopy, benches, lampposts, a plane tree, a kerb, and a guest waiting for a car. The right-hand third is still back-of-house — a service door, the chef on his break, crates, the dock and the van — because the crow needs one stretch of the block that is not a garden, and because the chef has to be standing in something.

And then it needed twice the room

The first version of the forecourt measured fine and played cramped. A fountain, a garden, an entrance and a loading bay inside fifty metres is a lot of objects per square metre, and the fixed camera cannot back off to compensate — so the only lever is space. The block went from ±25 to ±32, the fountain from 4.2 m to 3.2, and the garden from a continuous band round the water to four corner beds. Same contents, half the density.

The fountain came down off the roof

It spent a version of this level on the terrace as a plunge pool. That was a good excuse to prove the water code works at height — and it did, and the bug it found is in §9 — but it was bad level design: it put the dive, the money and both guards on the same deck, and left the ground floor with no reason to exist. A hotel puts its fountain at the front. So does this one.

The side effect is that the level now has a reason to send you down. The dive is at the bottom, the twenty is in the middle, the nest is at the top, and no single trip collects more than one of them.

§4 The money

$54.45 on the block against a $30 goal. Of that, $7.05 is unguarded and dry — and almost all of it is coins in a forecourt twelve metres under the nest. The endgame is the terrace, in every possible order of play, and no difficulty tuning produces that: the layout does.

The twenty is new to the game. Level 1's top prize was a ten; this is a twenty, and at two thirds of the goal the folded note in the maître d's bill-fold is the single most valuable object in either block.

§5 The four decks

The forecourt (y = 0)

Fountain, garden, canopy, benches, lamps — and a loading end. The cheapest money in the game is lying all over it, which is the joke: it is the most pleasant place on the block and the least worth your time.

The balconies (3.2)

There was a fire escape here. It made sense while the ground floor was a service yard and stopped making sense the moment it became a forecourt — hotels do not put fire escapes across their frontage, and it was the first thing a playtester objected to once the courtyard was pleasant.

Seven shallow Juliet balconies replaced it and do its three jobs better: a rest on the way up, somewhere to leave a coin, and — strung along sixty metres at 3.2 m — the thing that tells a player standing on the ground that this block goes up. The fire escape said the same thing by being an eyesore.

Each is railed on all three open sides. A rail across the front only reads as a bar somebody left there; a balcony is an enclosure, and the two short returns into the wall are what make it one.

The terrace (5.4)

Four fifths of the money, two guards, the kid, six gulls and five tables. Its parapet is a wall to a walker and a perch to a crow, and it is unbroken now — the gap in it existed so that a crow arriving up the fire escape did not have to clear a 0.7 m lip with its 0.34 m scramble, and with the fire escape gone nobody arrives on foot. It also has doors: one into the upper storey, one on the plant room. A terrace with staff on it and no way in says everybody climbed the fire escape.

The kid, and the problem of being one figure among five

She has had three homes and the first two were both wrong. On the fire-escape landing she was standing on the most awkward surface on the block, for something that is core loop. Moved to the terrace she was legible in the source and invisible on screen: a slightly shorter adult between a maître d', a busser and two diners, and a playtester could not tell who to hand a bottle cap to.

She is now sitting on the roof edge with her feet over the courtyard, at the end of the terrace furthest from the restaurant, with her cup and a row of trinkets beside her. Nobody else in either block sits down — it is the only posture in the game that is not a variation on standing — so the silhouette does the identifying before you are close enough to see what she is holding. The trinkets do the rest: a person on a wall with a line of bottle caps next to her is visibly the person who collects bottle caps, which the game can say without a marker or a tooltip.

Chimney Row (9.2), and the tank (12.35)

Pots, an aerial, the water tank with the nest on its lid, and deliberately nothing else. There was a pigeon loft with a feed tin on it; it was clutter on the one deck that should read as the crow's own, and the tin hung off the edge of the roof. Both are gone. The lid is round and its collider is the square inside it, so the crow can never stand on a corner with nothing underneath — and at 3.35 m against a 1.5 m twig ring it clears the nest-platform rule with room to spare, which matters more here than on the memorial because you arrive at this one out of breath.

§7 The gulls, and why they are allowed

A roof has no cover on it. On the block you could put a plane tree between yourself and the waiter, or take the awning, or drop behind the fountain. On a fifty-metre terrace there is nothing to put anywhere, and a level whose whole pitch is "the money is up here" cannot also be a level where being up here is free.

So the danger is authored as birds. Six gulls hold fixed pitches — three on the parapet, well clear of the kid, one by the plant room, two on the roof. Land inside one's patch and it shrieks; every person within eleven metres turns to look and gets a shove of suspicion. Fly over it and nothing happens, because a gull does not care about a bird two metres above its head and because punishing flight would punish the one thing the level is trying to teach.

They are markers that say not here. Unlike a guard they cannot be outrun — only avoided, or moved.

What this actually cost Gull extends Pigeon. A different mesh, a smaller wander range, and eleven lines that make a noise and hand the game a callback. The alarm runs through the same lookAt / _suspicion plumbing a caw already used — which is the point: a gull is the crow's own trick, used against it.

The joke it settles

This project exists because of an Instagram "would you rather" with two options: turned into a crow until you collect $20, or turned into a seagull until you eat 40 chips. The crow won, on the argument that $20 is a value ladder and 40 chips is 40 identical pickups. The seagulls got in anyway. So did the chips.

§8 The set piece

Level 1's marquee puzzle is a hot dog: drop it away from the cart, the pigeons mob it, the vendor leaves, and there are twelve seconds of unguarded ten. This block runs the same machinery and gets two effects out of one action.

There is a paper cone of chips on the fourth table. Drop it on the terrace, eight metres or more from the maître d's stand, and:

  • every gull on the terrace leaves its pitch for the chips — so an entire approach to the bill-fold goes quiet;
  • the maître d' goes to deal with them — so the thing at the end of that approach is unguarded.

Drop it in the forecourt instead and the fountain pigeons get a meal and the terrace carries on exactly as it was. That failure is the level's one deliberate teaching beat, and it is taught by failing rather than by a toast that arrives before you have done anything: birds do not use stairs. It is the cheapest possible way to tell a player that this block has floors.

And the weight

A candle lantern sits on the check on the third table. SHOVE it and the five comes loose. It is the saltshaker, exactly — the verb is taught on the block for free and charged for here, on a table beside a man who is paid to notice.

§9 What a second floor cost the engine

Almost everything in the game quietly assumed y = 0. Four generalisations were needed, all small, all of which leave level 1 bit-for-bit identical because it passes 0 everywhere: walkers take a floor, people and birds carry a floorY, light pools take a y, and the water body has a floor as well as a surface.

Five real bugs

Three could only exist on a tall block. Two were always there and nobody had built the shape that exposes them.

  • The crow was thrown sideways off the fire escape. Reported from a playtest as "clipping — resets me outside the playable area", and it was three faults at once. The lateral collision passes ejected toward whichever face the sign of the velocity implied, which is the face you came from only if you were moving on that axis at all; they resolved any footprint overlap they could see, so flying straight along z produced a shove along x; and the head bonk ran after them, so rising into the underside of a platform read as being inside it. Three stacked landings 0.25 m thick triggered all three on nearly every climb, moving the bird up to 3.2 m and sometimes clean through the edge of the block. Now: nearer face, only the axis that caused the overlap, and bonk first. Worst single-frame move went from 3.24 m to 0.20 m.
  • Gulls floated off the roof. Also from the playtest. A bird's y is authored and never integrated — nothing with feathers falls — which was invisible for as long as every bird stood on a plaza, because a plaza is everywhere. Put one on a parapet 0.6 m deep and it walks calmly off the side and hovers over the forecourt. The deck is a leash now: a step that leaves it is undone and the bird is sent home.
  • The pool was a lobster pot, again. The crow's scramble test read c.bottom <= 0.01 — which means "at y = 0", not "on the ground". A rim standing on a terrace could not be climbed out of at any heading. It now measures from the crow's own feet, which says the same thing on every floor. The fountain's basin was a trap once before for a completely different reason; this is the second time and the last, because the rule is now about a relationship rather than about zero.
  • You could swim in a pool five metres over your head. inWater was "inside the ring and below the surface", which is true of the entire column of air underneath a raised pool.
  • Pathing ran out of steering. stepAround deflected a blocked step by up to 112°, enough to get round a fountain and not enough to get round a seven-metre van: as a walker slides along a wide face away from its target, the direction to the target keeps rotating until every deflection under 112° still points into the wall. The committed side then has nothing free, the other side does, and the walker turns round — the old shuffle, arrived at from the opposite direction.

The one that looked like z-fighting

Irregular horizontal smears along the whole sixty-four-metre frontage, reported from a playtest as textures clipping into one another. It is shadow acne: a long building casts a long shadow across a very large ground plane, and inside that cast shadow the depth comparison fails in bands. It only appears at some sun angles, which is why it survived a shipping pass — the block looks clean at noon and striped at four o'clock.

normalBias is the knob, because it offsets the shadow lookup along the surface normal and that is exactly what a raking light on a big flat receiver needs. 0.03 → 0.15; it is clean by 0.10 and the rest is margin. The diagnostic worth remembering is the cheap one: before hunting for overlapping geometry, turn the shadow map off and look again.

And one that only a small fountain has

A coin on the far side of a fountain has exactly one sightline to the camera, and on a 3.2 m basin with a 2.3 m centrepiece in it that sightline goes straight through the fountain's own stem and dish. The wishing coins are all on one side now, in the arc where the ray leaves outward, and there are five of them rather than six because a basin this size does not have room for six coins and a shiny with a beak-length between every pair. Level 1's basin is 5.2 m across and its coins sit further out than its bowl is wide, which is why none of this ever came up there.

The fountain went back to the ground afterwards, for design reasons that have nothing to do with either water bug. Both fixes stayed, and so did both assertions: the bug is in the crow, not in the level, and the next block that puts water on a roof should not have to find it again.

§10 Three new rules

The contract moved out of world/level.js and into world/rules.js, because a rule that lives inside the level it constrains is not a contract, it is a comment. Every rule now runs against both blocks.

#RuleWhy it exists
9No climb longer than maxUnbrokenClimb = 9 m with nothing to land on 60% of what a full bar buys, so it clears on a bar that is two thirds empty. This is why the level is a staircase.
11Every pickup, person and bird rests on a deck that exists — and stays on it Unfalsifiable on a flat block and the easiest mistake on a tall one: write a terrace coordinate, forget the offset, and it hangs in the air with nothing under it and no way to see that from the source.
12Nothing shoves the crow more than 0.55 m in a frame The clipping report, made falsifiable. Aimed at every collider with air underneath it, from eight headings at three heights.
13There is somewhere legal to drop the bait The set piece has a deck requirement and a distance requirement. If they ever conflict, the block's marquee puzzle is quietly unsolvable.
14Unguarded money plus every trade cannot reach the goal The kid's ladder is per-level now. Trading may soften the block; it may never replace it.
15Nothing is standing in the edge kerb The kerb runs the whole length of a block, so anything careless near the near edge ends up half-sunk in it. A bin was.
Exhibit D — rules 1–8 are level 1's, unchanged, and now enforced on both.

Rule 11 earned its place immediately: it failed on level 1, on the ten hanging in the hot dog vendor's apron pocket. That one is deliberate, so it now says so in the level data — hung: true — and it is the only exemption on either block.

What the kid pays, and why it is bounded

The trade ladder used to be a constant in main.js. It is level data now, because what a trade is worth depends on what it costs to make one: on the block the kid sits alone in a corner of a plaza and a trade costs a walk; here she is on the roof edge in the middle of a working restaurant terrace, and every shiny has to be carried up past a maître d' and a busser. Same verb, much worse odds, so it pays half again — $1.50 / $2.25 / $3.00 / $4.00.

That is a knob with an obvious failure mode: pay enough and the honest route beats the dishonest one, and a game about robbing a hot dog vendor becomes a game about finding bottle caps. So rule 14 bounds it rather than taste doing so — everything you can pick up without crossing anybody, plus every trade the kid will ever make, still falls short of the goal. Here that is $7.05 plus $14.75 against $30.

§11 The light, and what it caught

This block starts at dayStart = 0.42 and gets the back 58% of the sunset ramp. Same eight minutes, less daylight in them: the lamps catch at 4m08s here against 5m46s on the block, so dusk arrives while you are still working rather than as a consolation for being slow.

That meant time-of-day is no longer the same number as progress through the run, which is exactly the class of arithmetic that has bitten this project twice. The HUD's sun dial is now handed elapsed seconds separately, and the lamp-timing assertion converts the trigger on to each level's own clock before comparing it to the rank ladder — in both directions, as the existing note demands.

The navigability floor did its job

The block must stay playable after dark: median luminance ≥ 48, 5th percentile ≥ 24, measured off the rendered frame with the HUD hidden. It failed badly on the first measurement — 5th percentile of 6 — and the fix was not more light. It was four materials: a fifty-metre terracotta wall facing away from a sun that comes from behind the block; a ground floor one paving step too dark for the sake of a "wet asphalt" fiction; a navy van; and six unlit teal windows that measured darker in daylight than the shadow they sat in, which is also just wrong — glass seen from outside on a sunny afternoon is a mirror of the sky, not a hole.

Shipped figures, over six samples from two camera positions across the session: median 53–127, 5th percentile 27–49, and blue beats red in the frame average at dusk. The floors were not touched — lowering a shared threshold to make a new level pass is the failure mode this repo has a whole note about.

One level is built at a time, so nothing above is additive at runtime. The whole cost of a second block is what it adds to the bundle: 165.1 kB gzipped, against roughly 155 before — about 10 kB, nearly all of it geometry described in source. There is still no asset pipeline, nothing to download, and no new draw-call class: the gulls are pigeons, the pools are the same additive quads, and the terrace tables are the café tables from world/kit.js.

Where the shared parts live now world/kit.js holds anything a third block would otherwise copy-paste — tables, benches, lamps, bins, planters, the skyline, the nest, and the water body. A café table is kit. A war memorial with a nest on it is not. Level 1 was rebuilt on the kit first and re-verified before this block was written, so the refactor and the new level never had to be debugged at the same time. Do that again.

§13 Deliberately not here

  • How you get from one block to another. Selection is ?level=2 and main.js is the only thing that reads it. That is a seam, not a join, and it is left open on purpose.
  • Carrying anything between levels. No money, no rank, no state. Each block is a session.
  • The inside of the hotel. The name commits to there being one. That is a different level and a different set of problems — interiors mean ceilings, and this engine has never had one.
  • New verbs. Still none. If a later block needs one, it should be because that block's pacing demands it — not because this one set a precedent.
