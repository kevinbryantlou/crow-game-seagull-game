§0 The shape of the set

Eight minutes, one crow, one nest, and a sum of money before the light goes. That loop has not changed since the first block and it is not going to. What changes between levels is the geography, and what the geography charges you for.

The goal rises five dollars a block. That is deliberately a small step — one you feel in the last minute of a run and never in the first — because the difficulty is not meant to live in the number. It lives in what the level makes expensive.

A description, not a rule Each block so far has charged for something the earlier ones did not: nothing, then being watched, then altitude, then enclosure, then footing. That is an observation about the five that got built. It is not a constraint a sixth block has to satisfy, and it would be a bad rule — it would rule out a good level on a technicality.

§1 The five that exist

1 · Small Change — The block · $20 · one deck

A plaza, a café row and a cart corner, flat, starting at the top of the afternoon with the whole sunset ahead. It charges you nothing: every mechanic in the game is taught here and every district has a softer one next door. The nest is on a war memorial you have no business being on.

What it cost: the black-mesh bug that started the whole harness, and the fountain that could be walked into at three headings and climbed out of at none. Its water is still the object every later basin is a copy of. docs/design-brief.html

2 · The Park — The Green · $25 · two decks

Written third and slotted second, because the roofline read as too big a leap from the block. Its entire design constraint was to be a small step: one pavilion roof, 1.0 m of unbroken climb, no new verbs. What it spends instead of altitude is social pressure — three people standing round one cooler with $10 on and beside it, and no district next door to work instead.

What it cost: the lawn, three times. Painted in tree greens it failed dusk twice over; corrected to pale sage the daylight became a beach. It is the block that proved a large surface must be checked at both ends of the light ramp. docs/park-brief.html

3 · The Hotel (Outside) — The Vantage · $30 · four decks

Forecourt, balconies, terrace, roof — a staircase of decks with the nest twelve metres up on a water tank. It spends altitude: money is priced by the climb, and a penny in the forecourt costs more to bank than it is worth. One new entity, the gull, because a roof has no cover on it and the danger has to be authored as something.

What it cost: shadow acne read as z-fighting for a week, and the kid became invisible among adults until she was made to sit — which is the silhouette rule every later block has had to defend. docs/level-2-brief.html

4 · The Hotel (Inside) — The Atrium · $35 · three decks

The same building through a different door, and the only repeat setting in the game. One room, 44 × 24 m, four walls, every guard sharing it. It spends enclosure — and it is only playable because of the trade it pairs with: laterally there is nowhere to run, vertically nobody can follow. The nest sits over the middle of the room, so the safest place is also the most conspicuous.

What it cost: a playtest removed the ceiling, the chandelier and the front door in one round — all three the same note, that this camera sits above the room. It also has the game's only easter egg: a pianist who takes any coin and plays one of three pieces. docs/lobby-brief.html

5 · The Wharf — The Landing · $40 · five decks

A quay, one pier, and a scatter of things that float. The first block whose ground is not continuous, so it spends footing: water is not a wall and not a fail state, it is a cost — 45% speed, three seconds wet, and a wingbeat you had not planned. The nest is on a net loft standing in open water with no walking route to it.

What it cost: seven playtest rounds. It also pays off the joke the project is named for — the pier belongs to gulls, there are more of them than people, and the seagull option from the original "would you rather" turns up as the setting of the last block. docs/wharf-brief.html

§2 Three that do not exist

Arguments rather than plans. Each is written the way the briefs are: what it charges for, what it would cost, and the thing most likely to be wrong. The mocks are sketches for mood and layout — none of this is built, and the numbers are guesses.

The device the next two blocks are built on Each block's backdrop contains the next block. The hotel proved it by accident — level 3 is the building from outside, level 4 is the same building from within — and the wharf set it up again on purpose: there is a container ship sitting in its backdrop, out past the breakwater, for a whole level. Going aboard it is earned rather than arbitrary. Do it twice and it stops being a coincidence and starts being how this game moves: you go to the thing you have been looking at.
Mock — Level 6, aboard the container ship. Stacks make canyons; the gaps between them are the map.

6 · The Container Ship — currency: sightlines

The ship that has been sitting in the wharf's backdrop all along, boarded. The deck is a grid of stacked containers with alleys between them, and the alleys are the level: the ground is perfectly continuous and you still cannot see where anything is. Flying up shows you the whole board at once — and costs stamina, and puts you above the only cover there is. So the currency is information. Every other block has spent something about getting to the money; this one spends knowing where it is.

Why it works here and would not work anywhere else: this camera looks down at 38°, so tall geometry hides the floor behind it. On every previous block that has been a defect to design around — it is what took the lobby's ceiling out and what made the wharf's first backdrop read as floating containers. Here it is the mechanic. The risk is the same fact pointed the other way: canyons deep enough to hide the money will also hide the crow, and a player who cannot see their own bird has stopped playing. The honest guess is stacks two high, not four, with the third tier used only where the alley is wide enough to see into — and that wants a real frame before any geometry is drawn.

Mock — Level 7, the cruise terminal. The container ship sits in the backdrop, two docks over: where you were last level, now scenery.

7 · The Cruise Terminal — currency: traffic

Where the ship sails to, and the most improbable place the crow has been yet: a pool deck. Sun loungers, a buffet nobody is watching, a queue at the gangway, luggage stacked on the quay. The park spent social pressure with three people all looking at one cooler; this is the opposite number — thirty people looking at nothing, moving constantly, and money left on every surface because everybody here is on holiday and nothing has been stolen from them in their lives.

So the crowd is cover and obstacle at once, and it moves: a route that was clear ten seconds ago is a wall of people now, and the luggage is the only thing on the quay that stays put. That flips the guard model the whole game has run on — for four blocks a person has been a cone to avoid, and here a person is somewhere to hide. The cost is in stepAround: thirty walkers steering round each other is a great deal more pathing than any block has asked for, and the honest flag is that crowd density is a performance question before it is a design one. And the container ship sits two docks over in the backdrop — out of bounds, unreachable, and the place you spent the last level.

Mock — Company. A system rather than a setting: it could attach to any block in the set.

8 · Company — currency: competition

Another crow, and it wants the same money. It has its own nest, it takes what you leave on the ground, and it follows you when you are carrying. The first level where something on the map has goals rather than a patrol and a cone, and it makes every earlier mechanic re-read: bait becomes something you can use on a rival, the kid's trade becomes contested, and putting a coin down to do something else stops being safe. It is also the only one of these three that is a system rather than a place — it could be attached to any block in the set, which is either its best property or the reason it never gets built.

Much more expensive than the other two, and the one that could make the game worse. Every human here has an authored floorY and never leaves it — exactly what makes them cheap, predictable and fair. A rival crow needs the crow's own locomotion, pathing in three dimensions, and a decision layer about what to chase. Worse, the tone of this game is that nothing can really hurt you: there is no fail state and there is not going to be one. A rival that reliably beat you to things would break that, so it would have to be a thief and not an opponent — beatable, a little stupid, and funnier than it is threatening. Build it last, and be prepared to cut it.

Parked, with one finding worth keeping Two earlier proposals came out when the ship chain replaced them. The Night Market — stalls packing up on a timer, so the map shrinks as the run does; still the cheapest idea on this page and still available. The Basin — a dry dock with the nest at the bottom, spending descent. That one produced a number worth keeping whatever gets built: a pit's floor is hidden by its own near rim, and a 4 m-deep hole needs roughly 4.6 m of clear depth in front of it before the floor is visible at all. Which rules out a shaft in this engine, permanently.

§3 What any new block inherits

Whatever gets built next, these are true of it on day one. They are the expensive lessons, and every one of them is in CLAUDE.md because something broke.

  • The camera never moves. 38° down, 25° of yaw, 20° lens. The sightline travels toward +x, +y and +z from every point, so the taller a thing is, the nearer it must be to stay in frame. A great many level-design mistakes are that one fact in disguise.
  • Humans never leave the deck they are authored on. Cheap, predictable, and the thing the lobby's entire design is built on.
  • Shade is violet, never grey, flat shading everywhere, three tones per object, no textures, the crow is the darkest thing on screen, nothing is outlined.
  • A large surface is a light-rig decision before it is a colour one, and a saturated one is too — a saturated hue has two low channels and renders near-black in shade.
  • A surface can carry one grid. A second at a different spacing is not texture, it is noise.
  • Every water body is the same object with different numbers: coping at 0.62, surface 0.20 below it. Those two are fixed by a pair of assertions that hold hands, not by taste.
  • The dusk floors are floors. Median 48, 5th percentile 24. They have never been lowered to make a block pass and must not be.
