§1 Why the crow

Both prompts are collect-a-thons, but they are not equally interesting collect-a-thons. Forty french fries is forty identical pickups — the number goes up, and that is the whole arc. Twenty dollars is a value ladder: a penny and a ten-dollar bill are both "money," but one is lying in a gutter and the other is folded inside a hot dog vendor's apron.

That gap is the game. Every pickup becomes a risk-priced decision instead of a pickup, and the player does the arithmetic themselves — $14.75, need $5.25, the tip jar has singles but the busker's case has a five. The seagull version has no arithmetic in it.

Crows also arrive with verbs already attached. They cache food, they steal, they trade shiny objects with people who feed them, they mob animals larger than themselves, and they are conspicuously smart in a way that reads as intent rather than instinct. A seagull is a blunt instrument. A crow is a protagonist.

The one-line pitch A fixed-camera mischief sandbox about a cursed office worker with wings, a hoarding instinct, and until sundown to make rent on his own body.

§2 The core loop

One city block. One crow. One nest on top of the war memorial. Money only counts when it is in the nest, and the crow can carry exactly one thing at a time in its beak.

There are five blocks now — this one, a park, a hotel seen from outside and again from inside, and a working wharf — and everything in this section is still true of all of them. It is the same loop with the geography doing more and more of the pricing that denominations do here. See §12.

Exhibit A — the loop. Step 05 is the difficulty curve; it is produced by the level, not by a knob.

Why one item at a time

The single-carry rule does an unreasonable amount of work. It turns every grab into a commitment, it creates travel (the run home is where the game happens), it makes the nest meaningful, and it means a chase has real stakes — get shooed and you drop what you are holding, and it scatters.

Teaching it

No tutorial and no tooltips. The loop is taught in three beats, each fired at the moment it becomes something the player can act on:

  • COLLECT MONEY on start — there is loose change within sight of the spawn point, so it is actionable immediately.
  • TAKE IT TO YOUR NEST the first time money is picked up. Front-loading this at the title would be telling somebody how to use a thing they are not holding.
  • THE KID ON THE BENCH WILL TRADE FOR THAT the first time a shiny is picked up, because a shiny is worth nothing in the nest and nothing on screen says so.

The second beat names the nest but not its location, so a chevron points the way — visible only while carrying unbanked money, and gone permanently after the first successful stash. At that point the loop is demonstrably learned, and the game should stop explaining itself.

Why the difficulty curve is free

The block holds $33.95 of reachable money against a $20 target. That sounds generous until you look at where it is: exactly $0.75 can be pocketed without crossing anybody — ten pennies, five nickels, four dimes, lying in the open. Another $1.50 is at the bottom of the fountain. Everything else, all $31.70 of it, belongs to someone.

So the endgame is always the guarded stretch, in every possible order of play, and no difficulty tuning is needed to produce that. What the slack buys is choice: you can blow the tip jar entirely, never touch the busker, and still make rent off the cart — but you cannot make rent off the floor.

Verified, not asserted These figures are asserted by scripts/smoke.mjs, which builds the real level and fails the run if unguarded money alone could ever reach $20. The numbers on this page and the numbers in the build cannot drift apart without the test going red.

The kid on the bench

A child sits with a paper cup of coins and will trade. Bring her a shiny worthless object and she puts a coin straight into your beak — starting at a dollar and escalating, so the fourth trade is worth more than the first. She is the game's safety valve: a player who keeps blowing the big scores can still grind their way to twenty and never gets stuck. She is also the emotional center, and the reason the ending has a second beat.

Two details there are load-bearing. The reward starts at a dollar, not a quarter, because finding a shiny and carrying it across the block is real work and a quarter reads as an insult. And it goes directly into the beak rather than onto the bench: the crow's beak is necessarily empty at that instant — it has just handed over the shiny — so there is nothing to gain by making the player hunt for a coin on the ground, where a bench or a tree can hide it.

§4 Verbs and controls

Four verbs. That ceiling is set by the phone, not by the design — anything that needs a fifth button on a touchscreen is out. Everything a crow does has to be expressible as move, flap, beak, caw.

VerbDesktopTouchDoes
Move WASD / arrows Left thumb stick Hop-walk on the ground. Deliberately unhurried — the crow is fast in the air and comic on foot.
Flap Space (hold) Right button ① Tap to hop a ledge. Hold to climb, with stamina; release to glide. Altitude is the resource — you spend it to get home.
Beak J / E / Enter Right button ② Grab, drop, deposit, tug, peck. Context-sensitive; the prompt names the target so it is never a guess.
Caw K Right button ③ Turns nearby heads toward the sound. The only tool for moving a human without touching them. Overuse makes a person suspicious rather than curious.
Exhibit C — the full input surface. One stick, three buttons, no menus during play.
Design rule Caw is the puzzle verb, not a joke button. If a set piece can only be solved by walking into things, it is not finished.

Finding the controls again

A player who forgets which key takes a thing should never have to restart to find out, and a game with no fail state has no business owning a pause menu. So the scheme is taught in two places, neither of which stops play:

  • The beak prompt carries its own key. It already floats at the beak naming the verb and the object; it now reads [J] TAKE — DOLLAR BILL. The control is taught at the exact moment it is relevant, and costs no screen space at all.
  • A legend, bottom left. Open at the start, collapses itself to a [H] CONTROLS pill after twenty-five seconds, and toggles on H or ? forever after. Hidden on touch, where the buttons are already on screen and labelled.

§5 People

There is no combat and no damage. Humans are moving weather. Each one owns a thing, watches a wedge of space in front of them, and escalates through three states.

  • Calm — walks a route or stands. Ignores a crow on the ground.
  • Suspicious — you are inside their wedge and near their property. They turn, they say something, a small marker appears over them. Two seconds of grace.
  • Shooing — they come at you flapping a newspaper. Faster than you on foot, useless once you are airborne. Catching you knocks whatever you are carrying out of your beak, and the crow tumbles a few feet, feathers everywhere.

They cool down and go back to what they were doing, and they put their property back. Nothing is ever permanently lost — a botched heist costs time and dignity, which in a game with a sunset timer is exactly enough.

The cast

  • The waiter — patrols Café Row, the block's most aggressive patroller. Owns the tip jar.
  • The hot dog vendor — anchored to his cart, huge wedge, owns the ten. Leaves only for a genuinely good reason.
  • The busker — eyes closed while playing. Opens them between songs. Owns the five in the case.
  • The phone guy — obliviously walks a loop with an open bag. A moving pickup.
  • Pigeons — not a threat. They mob any dropped food and will happily ruin your distraction if you are careless with a hot dog.

§6 The block

One continuous space, not levels. Three districts flow into each other so the player is never loading, never choosing from a menu, and always able to see the next interesting thing from where they are standing.

Exhibit D — plan view. Money density falls left to right; guard density rises. The nest sits deliberately at the seam, so every run home crosses traffic.

Reading the map

Fountain Plaza teaches by being safe. Coins are visible from the spawn point, the fountain teaches diving, the memorial teaches climbing and doubles as the nest. Nobody chases you here.

Café Row introduces ownership. Tables have things on them and the waiter has opinions. It is a corridor, so escapes have to go vertical.

Cart Corner is the endgame. Scaffolding gives an aerial route that the vendor cannot follow, and the ten dollars sits under the one man on the block who never stops watching.

Rules any future block must obey

These are not style preferences. They are asserted against every built level by scripts/smoke.mjs — which runs scripts/audit-level.mjs once per entry in the level registry — so a new block cannot ship having quietly broken one.

There were eight when this section was written and there are seventeen now. The nine that arrived later all came from the same place: building further blocks and finding out which of the first block's assumptions were rules and which were coincidences. They live in world/rules.js rather than inside the level they constrain, because a rule that lives inside its own level is not a contract, it is a comment.

  • A nest's landing surface is at least twice the nest. Banking happens under pressure — it is the moment a chase resolves. The last obstacle between a player and their money must never be a pixel-accurate landing on a plinth the size of the nest itself. The nest can stay small; the roof it sits on cannot.
  • No two takeable things within one beak-length. The beak grabs whatever is nearest, so two pickups closer than about 1.2 units are not two targets — they are one ambiguous target, and the player experiences it as the controls failing. Loose change of the same denomination is exempt; it is meant to read as a single heap.
  • Nothing is built inside the fountain, or inside any other volume the player is meant to move through.
  • No pickup sits inside solid geometry. An object embedded in a collider cannot be reached at all, and nothing on screen says so.
  • Every place the crow can get into, it can get out of. The fountain basin failed this for the whole of its first life. Its rim was a circle approximated by twelve boxes that met only at their corners, so the crow could walk in from three headings out of a hundred and eighty — and because flapping was disabled in water, a crow that got in by any other route could never leave. Both halves are now asserted: the wall is continuous at every heading, and from twenty-four starting points on the basin floor the crow can always flap out.
  • Nobody stands inside anything, and no route walks through anything. People are solid against the block. Nobody spawns embedded in geometry, no patrol waypoint sits inside a solid, and no patrol route crosses the fountain. The newsagent stood inside his own magazine rack for weeks, which reads from the fixed camera as a head growing out of a shelf.
  • The block stays navigable after dark, and the sunset is reachable. Measured on the rendered frame, not eyeballed: median and 5th-percentile luminance have floors, and the hour the street lamps catch must fall after a fast run ends and well before the day does. Both halves were broken at once — the light was too dark and the day was so long that nobody reached it.
  • A chase can get round every large solid. Shooing steers straight at the crow, and the crow can stand behind whatever it likes, so no route authoring can cover this. Pushing a walker out of a wall they are walking into just puts them back where they were: they have to be able to go around.

The nine added since, in the order they were needed:

  • No climb longer than nine metres with nothing to land on. Sixty per cent of what a full stamina bar buys, because nobody arrives at the bottom of a climb with a full bar — you arrive having just flown away from someone.
  • Everything rests on a deck that exists, and stays on it. Pickups, people and birds. Unfalsifiable on a flat block and the easiest mistake on a tall one: write a terrace coordinate, forget the offset, and it hangs in the air with nothing under it and no way to see that in the source. Birds needed the second half — nothing with feathers falls, so a gull put on a parapet walks calmly off the side and hovers.
  • Nothing shoves the crow more than half a metre in a frame. The collision passes used to eject toward the face the sign of the velocity implied and resolve any overlap they could see, which threw the bird three metres sideways off anything with air underneath it.
  • There is somewhere legal to drop the bait. The set piece has a distance requirement and, on a block with floors, a deck requirement. If they ever conflict, the marquee puzzle is quietly unsolvable.
  • Unguarded money plus every trade cannot reach the goal. What the kid pays is per-block now, because what a trade is worth depends on what it costs to make one. That knob has an obvious failure mode — pay enough and the honest route beats the dishonest one — so it is bounded rather than eyeballed.
  • Nothing is standing in the edge kerb. It runs the whole length of a block, so anything careless near the near edge ends up half-sunk in it.
  • Every plane lying on another surface is a depth decal, and no two of them overlap at the same height. Two rules, one bug, three rounds to find: paving patches at identical depth are a coin flip per pixel and draw a staircase across the whole frontage. Polygon offset cannot separate two things it nudges equally.
  • The lamps catch on this block's own clock. A block may start partway into the afternoon, so the trigger has to be converted before it is compared to anything — the same "right fraction of the wrong day" arithmetic that shipped the eighteen-minute sunset.
Where these came from Every one of these was a real defect found in playtest or in a harness, not a precaution. The first cost a run: a panicked landing during a chase missed a 2.0-unit plinth by a few pixels and fell off the ledge. The most recent was reported three times before it was correctly diagnosed, and two of the fixes in between were right about the symptom and wrong about the mechanism.

§7 Shape of a session

There is no story mode. There is a block, a sunset, and a short list of things a crow could try — the Untitled Goose Game arrangement, because it is the correct one for a sandbox with four verbs.

This said "and there are no levels" for as long as there was one block. There are two now, and the sentence was worth keeping honest rather than quietly deleting: a session is still one block, one sunset, no save. Nothing carries between them — not money, not rank, not state — and both endings are written to stand alone. What exists is a second sandbox, not a campaign.

The list

A handful of tasks sit in the corner of the screen. They are suggestions, not requirements; the money is what matters, and the list exists to point at set pieces the player might not have noticed. Sample: Rob the tip jar · Dive for the wishing coins · Trade something shiny · Make the vendor leave his cart · Get the ten · Take a hat.

Sundown, and the second clock

The sun is the only timer, and for most of the session it is the only pressure. The day lasts eight minutes. It lasted eighteen for a long time, which was nine times a competent run and meant the light did not visibly change until 10m48s — so nobody who played well ever saw dusk, and the sunset the game is named around never happened. At eight, the lamps catch at 5m46s: finish well and daylight is the reward, take your time and the sunset is. The light rig runs continuously off elapsed / SESSION_SECONDS — the key light swings twenty degrees across the block, drops toward the horizon and goes from warm gold to violet, and the sun dial in the corner of the HUD is the only piece of UI that reports it.

That was not enough. At about 70% of the day the block used to stop being readable: its median luminance fell to 19/255. The last third of the session — which is the guarded stretch, the part the money layout in §3 deliberately saves for the end — was the least playable part of the game.

So the block now comes on. At 72% of the day, while there is still light, four streetlamps catch a beat apart with a sodium flicker, the city fills in behind them, and the café, both stalls, the scaffolding and the fountain follow over the next few seconds. This is a design element, not a rendering one, for three reasons:

  • It is a second clock. The sun dial is a widget in the corner. The lamps coming on is the same information delivered by the world, and it is the more frightening version — you look up because the street changed, not because you were watching a gauge.
  • It arrives before the problem, not after. Real streetlights come on while it is still light and look faintly pointless for ten minutes. Light that shows up once the player is already lost reads as a fix; light that shows up just before reads as a world.
  • It changes what the last third is. Late in the day the block becomes points of warmth in violet shade, and the glints on the money are among the brightest things left. The endgame turns into a treasure hunt by glint without a single rule changing.
Exhibit · the plaza at 95% of the day, with a prototype ground pool under the lamp · see docs/lighting-brief.html

Two rules constrain all of it. Shade is violet, never grey — so the colour that lights every surface the sun no longer reaches swings violet as the sun drops, which is also what makes warm street lighting read as warm. And the crow is the darkest thing on screen, which is how the player finds the crow, so light may fall around it and not on it: the bird is silhouetted in a pool of lamplight rather than lit by one.

Full treatment, including what was measured and what is still undecided, is in docs/lighting-brief.html.

Ranks

The first version graded on the clock alone, with thresholds guessed before anyone had played. The first competent playtest run came in at 2m 07s — so "under five minutes" was not a distinction, it was the default, and every informed run collected the top title.

The clock is now tuned to that data, and more importantly the title mostly describes how rather than how fast. A twelve-minute run in which you were never once caught is a better story than a quick clumsy one, and it used to score worse. Each title answers a different question, so replaying differently earns a different name instead of a higher number.

Sundown

A real-time clock runs the light from mid-afternoon gold to low orange to blue dusk over about eighteen minutes. It is not a fail timer — reaching dusk without twenty dollars just means the light is beautiful and the crow is still a crow, and the player can keep going. It closes out the session with a rank instead of a loss.

Ending Drop the coin that crosses $20 into the nest and the transformation starts — and the first thing the newly human player sees is the kid on the bench, still holding out a bottle cap for a bird that is no longer there. That is the whole story. It does not need a cutscene.

The ending headline is the amount, spelled out and delivered a word at a time — TWENTY-TWO DOLLARS / SIXTY-SIX CENTS. A figure is a receipt; a sentence is what it cost you. The rank line carries the time and the title only, since repeating the number under it would be noise.

§8 Camera

Fixed high-angle, slightly side-on, never rotates. Roughly 38° of pitch and 25° of yaw off the world axis, on a near-orthographic lens — long focal length, so the block reads as a diorama and parallel lines stay parallel.

It follows the crow with a soft dead zone and a lag of about a fifth of a second, and it eases up and out when the crow gains altitude so that climbing literally shows you more of the board. That is the one concession: the angle is fixed, the distance is not, because a flying character on a locked-distance camera either flies off screen or never gets to feel like it is flying.

Anything between the camera and the crow — an awning, a truck — fades to a silhouette rather than being clipped away, which keeps the diorama intact.

§9 Build and the iOS path

WebGL through three.js, no engine, no asset pipeline. Every model in the game is generated from primitives in code, which means the whole build is a few hundred kilobytes, loads instantly, has nothing to 404, and can be recolored globally by editing one palette file.

What makes it portable on day one

  • Touch is not a retrofit. The input layer is written as one abstract state object; keyboard and the on-screen stick both write into it, and the game never asks which one is active.
  • Portrait and landscape both work. Layout is driven by safe-area insets from the start, so a notch changes nothing.
  • Fixed-step simulation at 60 Hz with a decoupled render, so a 120 Hz iPad and a throttled phone produce identical physics.
  • No network, no storage beyond localStorage, no third-party scripts — which is also what makes App Store review boring, and boring is the goal.
  • Capacitor wrap is then genuinely a config file and an Xcode build: npx cap add ios over the existing dist/.

Performance budget

Under 150 draw calls, a single directional light with one shadow map, no post-processing beyond a cheap grade. Target 60 fps on an iPhone 12; the block is small enough that this is not ambitious.

§12 The other blocks

Four of them now, and the useful way to read the set is that each one spends a different currency. This section is only what the reader of this document needs to know; each block has its own brief.

  • The Park — level 2, two decks, $25, spends social pressure. docs/park-brief.html
  • The Hotel (Outside) — level 3, four decks, $30, spends altitude. docs/level-2-brief.html (the filename predates the slot).
  • The Hotel (Inside) — level 4, three decks, $35, spends enclosure: one room, every guard sharing it, and safety only ever upward. docs/lobby-brief.html
  • The Wharf — level 5, five decks, $40, spends footing: the first block whose ground is not continuous. docs/wharf-brief.html

The ladder is $20 / $25 / $30 / $35 / $40 — five equal steps, one rung per block — and one deck / two / four / three inside one room / five with water between them. Both rows are deliberate. The second rung exists because the gap between the block and the roofline was measured and found too big, which is why the park was written third and slotted second.

That each block spends something different is the whole of the progression. It is not a difficulty curve and deliberately not one: the goal rises by five dollars a block, which is a step you feel in the last minute of a run and never in the first. What changes is what the level charges you in — a district next door, a climb, a room with no exit, a floor that is not continuous. A sixth block that spends more of any of those is a bigger version of a block that already exists.

The argument for building upward is that everything in §1–§5 stays true and one thing changes. The block is flat, and the crow's wings, stamina bar, glide and list of perches are barely load-bearing — you can play a competent 2m07s run and never climb higher than the memorial you bank on. So the roofline spends the system the first one installed and never used: altitude. The nest is twelve metres up, money density rises with the floor, and a penny in the forecourt costs more to bank than it is worth. No new verbs. One new entity, a gull, because a roof has no cover on it and the danger has to be authored as something.

The park spends a different budget. It has one roof and 1.0 m of unbroken climb, so altitude is introduced as a fact rather than as a problem; what it charges for instead is being watched. Its marquee is three people standing round one cooler with $10 on and beside it, where this block always gave you a softer district next door. Again no new verbs — one new noun, a soft pretzel, which turned the bait vocabulary from a hardcoded pair into a set the audit reads.

What it cost this document to be true of both

Almost everything quietly assumed y = 0: walker collision, where people and birds stand, where a light pool lands, and what counts as being in water. All four now take a floor, and the block passes zero everywhere and is unchanged. The level-specific things that used to be constants in main.js — the goal, the task list, the teaching toasts, the set-piece rules, the trade ladder, the ending copy — are level data now, in world/levels.js. Shared props are in world/kit.js: a café table is kit, a war memorial with a nest on it is not.

Still open

How a player gets from one block to the next — half answered. Finishing a block now hands you the next one: the ending screen carries a brass button naming where it goes, with a replay beside it in outline. Losing offers only the replay, because the rule is that you reach a block by finishing the one before it. The whole progression system is a next field on the level descriptor.

What that bought, besides discoverability, is that a block is now built and torn down inside a live page instead of by reloading it — which is what "Again!" used to do, and why it dropped you back on the title card. A reload also silences the game: the audio context can only be created from a user gesture and a fresh page has not had one yet.

Still open is the menu: there is no way to reach an arbitrary block, no record of what you have finished, and no route backwards. That needs saved state, which nothing in the game has yet. ?level=N still works and is still the seam.

Whether the park is a big enough step — settled. The roofline's slot was the first open question in this section, and it got answered: a playtest read it as too big a leap to follow the block directly, so its goal came down to $30 and the park was written to sit between them. The park has now been played too, and it reads as solid — the only block of the three that has not needed a revision round. Its pavilion and its single 3.4 m deck stand as built, which also answers the worry that one roof would leave the roofline as steep as before. It does not.

The goal held too: $25 reads as an endgame rather than a grind, so the $20 / $25 / $30 ladder is confirmed at all three rungs. What is left is one question, and it needs somebody playing for a time rather than for a look — whether the park's picnic reads as a problem to plan around before it becomes one. docs/park-brief.html §9 keeps the list, closed items included.
