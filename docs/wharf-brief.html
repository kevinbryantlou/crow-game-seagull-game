§0 Where this stands

Proposed, and under construction. This document was written before any geometry existed, which is the same order the last three briefs were written in and the reason they were worth anything — the argument has to exist first so the build can be checked against something rather than described afterwards.

It is written to be vetoed. §10 is the short list of decisions worth stopping, ranked, with the cost of reversing each one. Two of them touch shared code and one of them changes a rule that has been true since level 1, so they are the ones to read first if you read nothing else.

In one sentence Level 5 spends footing. Every block so far has a floor you can walk the whole of; this one does not, so every route across it is priced in wingbeats and every mistake is priced in a swim.

§1 What a fifth block is for

Four blocks exist and each spends a different currency. The block spends nothing — it is the tutorial with a sunset on it. The park spends social pressure. The roofline spends altitude. The lobby spends enclosure: one room, no district next door, and safety only ever upward.

Those four cover a lot, and between them they cover everything about who is looking at you and how far up you have to go. What none of them has touched is the ground itself. On all four blocks the floor is a continuous surface: you can put the stick in any direction and walk there. Flight is a shortcut and an escape hatch, and it is never the only way to get somewhere.

So the wharf breaks the floor. The walkable world is a quay along the near edge, one pier running out into the water, and then a scatter of things that float — two docks, two boats, a dinghy, a row of pilings, and a harbor light with the nest on it. Between them is water.

The trade this block makes Water is not a wall and it is not death — this game has no fail state and is not getting one. It is a cost. In the water you move at 45% speed, you are soaked for three seconds after you get out, and getting out is a beat of the wings you did not plan to spend. Missing a landing costs you eight seconds, never a run.

Why a wharf, specifically

Two reasons, and the second one is the one I would defend if only one survived.

The first is that a fishing dock is the only ordinary place where discontinuous ground is not a contrivance. A construction site or a fire escape would give the same geometry and would have to explain itself; a harbor is a place everyone has already been to where the floor is genuinely a set of islands, and nothing about it needs a reason.

The second is that this game exists because of a choice between two jokes. The Instagram "would you rather" offered a crow with twenty dollars and a seagull with forty fries, and this is the crow one — chosen because $20 is a value ladder and 40 fries is 40 identical pickups. Four blocks in, the fifth is a pier that belongs to gulls. They are on the pilings, on the shed ridge, on the beacon rail, and there are more of them than there are people. You are a crow working a dock that is already somebody's.

That is a callback rather than a mechanic, and it costs nothing: gulls already exist as entities, level 3 already mobs them with food, and the only new noun on this block is the fish. But it makes the fifth block mean something in a way that a fifth new district would not, and it does it without a word of copy explaining the joke.

No new verbs Fly, take one thing, bank it, shove the weight off a bill, bait a guard with food, trade a shiny with a kid. All six are level 1's. The only new noun is a mackerel, a fifth member of BAIT_KINDS — which the audit reads as a vocabulary rather than a list, so nothing else has to notice.

§2 The plan

Read it as a letter U turned on its side. The quay wraps the water on three sides — along the bottom of the frame, up the west end and up the east end — and the water fills the middle and the top. One pier runs out from the quay into it. Everything the level asks you to do happens either on the quay or out on the water, and the whole design is about the seam between those two.

Putting the water at the back is not decoration. The camera looks up-screen at 38°, so a sightline from anything on the map leaves toward +x and +z — which means anything standing out in the basin has nothing but open water between it and the lens. Money over water is unhideable by construction, which is the only reason it is safe to put a five on a piling nine metres from anywhere.

Exhibit A — the wharf in plan. The camera is fixed at bottom right, looking up-screen. Roughly 15 px to the metre. The block is x −30…30, z −15…15.

The five decks, and how you climb them

The vertical story is not a staircase of buildings like the roofline's. It is a staircase of boats, and it only exists out over the water:

  • 0 — the quay. Everybody in the cast except one, and every building.
  • 0.62 — the pier, level with the coping, reached from the quay by two 0.31 m steps at its root. Under the crow's 0.34 scramble, so you can walk out; under a walker's 0.45 step, so the deckhand can too.
  • 1.15 — the charter boat's deck. The skipper stands on it and cannot leave it, which is a guard on an island.
  • 2.4 — the wheelhouse roof.
  • 3.4 — the beacon's gallery, and 6.5 the crown with the nest in it.

The tallest unbroken climb to the nest is 3.1 m against a 9 m rule, which is deliberately generous — the difficulty on this block is not vertical. What the route actually costs is the horizontal gap between the pier and the beacon, crossed with nothing underneath you, on whatever bar you have left after flying away from somebody.

The nest is a lighthouse, and that is the level's thesis in one object It stands in the water with no walking route to it at all. Every trip to bank anything is a flight out over open water and back — which is the same shape as the lobby's flight up through the middle of the room, rotated ninety degrees. It is also the block's one lit thing after dark, the way the chandelier was the lobby's, and a harbor light is the only object in this game whose job is to come on at dusk.

The kid's ladder

tradeValues: [2.00, 3.00, 4.50, 5.50] — $15.00 across four shinies, a rung above the lobby's. She is the easiest kid in the game to reach: she is on a crate at the water's edge in the middle of the quay, and nobody owns the ground around her. What she costs is that two of the four shinies are out over the water — one on the east float, one on the wheelhouse roof — so half her ladder is paid for in the same currency as everything else here.

Bounded by the rule rather than by taste: unguarded money plus every trade she will ever make is $27.50 against $40, so trading can soften this block by more than half and can never finish it. smoke asserts that, not me.

$40, and the case for $35

The ladder is $20 / $25 / $30 / $35 and the honest fifth term is $40. That is not much of an argument on its own — the lobby was built at $40 on a fiction argument and came down to $35 on a playtest note about the run, and the run won. So here is both sides, because you overruled this exact number once already.

For $40. The reason $40 failed on the lobby was that the lobby is one room with nowhere else to work: when the desk is hot you wait, and waiting at $40 is a grind. The wharf is the opposite shape. It has four separate pitches — the market, the ice house, the harbormaster's window, the boat — plus $8.50 sitting out on the water that nobody guards. There is always something else to be doing, which is the property the lobby lacked and the exact property that makes a bigger number survivable.

For $35. Two blocks in a row that both raise the ask and add a new cost is two lessons at once, which is the mistake the park was written to correct. And the new cost here is specifically time: a round trip to the beacon is longer than a climb to a chandelier, and you pay it on every single bank. If $40 grinds, it will grind for exactly the reason the lobby did, arriving by a different road.

I am building $40 because the block has districts and the lobby did not. It is one line in levels.js and the geometry does not care — see §10.

§4 The fish market

Every block has one set piece the level is arranged around: the hot dog cart, the picnic cooler, the restaurant terrace, the front desk. Here it is the market shed — $29.00 across one counter, with the fishmonger standing at the open end of it where the camera can see him. That last part is the park's lesson and the lobby's: a guard whose leaving is the puzzle has to be unmistakably visible both before and after he leaves.

Three problems in one place, solved in different orders by different players:

  • The tip jar ($4.00) is a reach over the counter at the far end from him. Cheap, and it teaches the counter's geometry for free.
  • The five under the tackle box ($5.00) needs a SHOVE first — this block's saltshaker, its paperback, its candle lantern, its brass bell. Two beats, on the cutting table, in front of him.
  • The day's take ($20.00) is in an open cash box on the counter, and he does not move for anything except birds on his floor.

Which is the mackerel. Drop it on the quay more than 8 m from the counter and every gull on the wharf goes for it, and he leaves the box to drive them off. Drop it out on the water and nothing happens — the gulls eat, and he does not care, because the water is already theirs and it is not his floor. That is the roofline's birds do not use stairs rule wearing the only hat this block could give it, and this block is late enough to be allowed to assume the lesson rather than teach it.

Why the mackerel and not more fries A cone of fries is level 3's bait and its toast already reads "Chips. Gulls like chips." — which is a British word in player-facing copy and is on the list of things you have told me not to do. See §11: I found it while writing this and it is a two-word fix on a shipped block, so it is yours to call rather than mine to slip in.

§5 The silhouette, and the debt I am not spending

The kid is identified by her silhouette: she sits, and until level 4 nothing else in the game sat. The roofline invented that, the park defended it against being mostly benches, and the lobby spent it — the pianist sits, so the rule is now the weaker "nothing else sits in the open".

I inherit the weaker rule and I am not weakening it further. Nobody on this block sits down except the kid. That takes defending, because a fishing dock is full of people sitting on bollards with a line in the water — so:

  • The two people fishing off the east quay stand at the rail.
  • The deckhand walks, the fishmonger stands at his counter, the harbormaster stands at his window, the skipper stands on his boat.
  • There are two benches on the quay and both are empty, exactly as the park's four are.

She sits on an upturned crate at the edge of the quay — a shape nothing else on the block has, low and hard and nowhere near the furniture — and she is placed by the park's rule rather than the roofline's: on the camera side of the water, so the water is behind her. A small figure against teal is a small figure you can see. And she is nowhere near the crab pots, which are this block's deliberate near-side occluder: an occluder only fades on the one ray between camera and crow, and trading is core loop.

If a playtest is ever confused about who trades The lobby's brief says the pianist is what goes. That is still the right answer and this block does not change it — but it is worth writing down that after two blocks of paying it down, the margin is one seated easter egg wide.

§6 The water, and what the engine actually allows

This is the part of the proposal most likely to be wrong, so it is the part with the arithmetic in it. I went looking for what a big body of water would cost before designing around one, and the answer changed the design twice.

The water in this game has always been a circle

world.fountain is { x, z, r, rim, floor } and every test of it is hypot(x−f.x, z−f.z) < f.r − 0.7. There are exactly three such tests in the shipped game: two in crow.js (is the bird swimming, and where is the basin floor under it) and one in pickups.js (does a dropped coin land on the basin floor). A harbor is not a circle, and four circular ponds in five blocks is the same object five times.

So the spec gains a shape, the predicate moves into world/collide.js as a pure function beside overlaps(), and the three call sites read it instead of doing the maths inline. Levels 1–4 pass no shape, take the circle branch, and are bit-for-bit unchanged — which is the same shape of change as the floor parameter the roofline added to every walker function.

What this does not need A new collider shape. The ring exists because a circular wall has no axis to slide along; a rectangular basin's coping is four ordinary boxes, and the crow's existing box logic already does the right thing at both ends. That was the single biggest risk in this design and it evaporated on reading the collision code.

The vertical geometry is not a choice

Two assertions in audit-level.mjs hold hands, and between them they fix every height in a water body to within a couple of centimetres:

  • "the rim is a wall at every heading — you go in over the top, not through", which walks a crow at the water from 180 directions and fails if any of them gets in.
  • "a crow in the water can simply walk out, no flapping at all", which walks a crow at the rim from 24 directions inside and fails if any of them is trapped.

The crow scrambles onto anything within STEP_UP = 0.34 m of its feet, and afloat its feet are at the water surface rather than wherever the bob has it. So the coping has to stand more than 0.34 above the land (or you fall in by walking) and less than 0.34 above the water (or you are in a lobster pot). The shipped numbers — coping 0.62, surface 0.20 below it — are the only pair that satisfies both, and that is why the fountain, the plunge pool, the duck pond and the lobby basin are all the same object with different radii.

The consequence for a harbor is that the water sits 0.42 m above the quay. I want to be straightforward that this is a compromise rather than a happy accident. What saves it is the camera: at 38° from thirty metres you see a quay, a low stone lip, and water beyond it, and the park's pond is that exact object and has never been remarked on. It also happens to be what a harbor looks like at full tide, when the water is lapping the top of the quay wall — so the block is set at high tide and everything that floats sits high.

The alternative was to make the drop from quay to water a real one and let the crow walk off the edge, which reads better and breaks the first of those two assertions on purpose. I am not doing that. It is a rule that has been true since level 1, the block does not need it to work, and "we weakened a safety assertion so the water would look better" is how the fountain became a lobster pot twice.

Things standing in the water, which is the one rule I am changing

The audit currently says nothing is built inside the water, full stop. It is a good rule and its reason is right — don't put obstacles in a volume the player swims through. But a wharf without pilings under it is not a wharf, and the pier, both floats, two boats and the beacon all stand in the basin by definition.

So the binary rule becomes a declared one, in the same style as hung: true on the roofline's pickup: a collider may carry inWater: true, which is a thing somebody has to type into the level source and which the audit prints back by name. And in exchange the escape test gets stronger, because a declared exemption is only safe if the thing it protects is measured:

  • Today the escape test samples the middle of the basin and two radii out from it — 8 headings × 3 radii, which is 24 points in a circle.
  • It will sample a grid across the whole basin, skip the cells that are inside a declared solid, and require every remaining cell to get the crow out — holding flap, tapping flap, full bar and empty bar, all four, as now.

That is strictly more coverage than levels 1–4 get today, and it runs on all five blocks. A rule that can quietly end up with nothing to check is not a rule, so it also asserts it found open water to check.

§7 The light, and the debt I am inheriting

The block starts at dayStart: 0.58, so RULES.lampsOnAt (0.72) arrives at 2 m 40 s of the eight-minute day, against the lobby's 3 m 01 s, the roofline's 4 m 08 s and the block's 5 m 46 s. One more rung, and a small one: there is not much afternoon left to spend, and the point of the ladder is that it is felt rather than measured.

The inherited number that worries me is not luminance. It is hue. The lobby ships with blue beating red by 10 and 17 at t = 0.98, down from 18 and 34, because warm additive light eats the violet the whole style guide is built on — and the dusk floors will keep passing long after the frame stops looking right, because they only measure brightness.

The one place this block is lucky Its biggest surface is water, and water is the only large surface in this game that is cool by nature. If it is painted right, the wharf should pay some of that debt back rather than spend more of it — a big cool plane under a warm sky is exactly what the violet needs to read against.

Painted wrong it does the opposite, and the trap is well documented: the park's lawn is the biggest surface in the game and took three attempts, failing dusk at a saturated green and going to a beach at a pale one. A saturated teal would swallow the violet the same way a saturated green did. So the water gets its own palette entry — pale, cool, high value, well clear of the 5th-percentile floor — and it gets measured at t = 0 and t = 0.98, never one or the other.

Fixtures: a wharf is lit by pole lights on the quay, the market's strip lighting, the ice house door, and the beacon. The beacon is the event. Everything else follows the rule that has now been stated four times — light the ground, not the lamp count — so the quay decals go pale before a single extra pole goes in, and the pool areas stay small and bright rather than large and dim, because a pool's cost is its area and its brightness is nearly free.

§8 What changes outside this level

  • world/wharf.js — new. Named for the place, like park.js and lobby.js. Slot numbers are a registry question, not a filename one, and no existing level file gets renamed.
  • world/levels.js — a fifth descriptor, and level 4's next goes from null to 5. That one edit is the entire unlock: the ending screen grows a brass The wharf → button, the Levels screen grows a fifth chip, and save.js opens the door.
  • world/collide.js — the water predicate, as a pure function. entities/crow.js and world/pickups.js — three call sites, now reading it.
  • scripts/audit-level.mjs — the water rules generalised, and the escape grid. Runs on all five blocks.
  • scripts/shoot.mjs — a level 5 section at 60–69 and l5-dusk-*. Not 40–49: 40 is the touch pause control, and two sections writing one filename is a screenshot that quietly stops being the thing it is named after.
  • render/palette.js — the harbor water, plus the matching swatches in docs/style-guide.html. The doc is the spec, so they change together.
  • world/pickups.js — the mackerel mesh and label, and fish added to BAIT_KINDS. The audit reads that set rather than naming kinds, so nothing else has to notice.

What is not being changed: the lobby's ending copy. It was written as the game's last ending and it closes rather than points — but it never names a next place, and the button under it does the pointing, which is the same reasoning that left the roofline's ending alone when the lobby arrived. If you would rather it pointed, that is a copy call and a small one. Flagged, not done.

§9 The words

Tasks

  • Dive the harbor
  • Get the five off the far pilings
  • Trade something shiny
  • Get the fishmonger away from his counter
  • Get the twenty

The second one is the block's teaching line and it is doing the most work: it names a place you can see from the quay, and the only way to obey it is to fly out over water. That is the level in one sentence, delivered as a chore.

Toasts

  • money — Take it to your nest
  • shiny — The kid on the crate will trade for that
  • bait — A mackerel. Every gull on this pier is already looking at you.
  • pin — The five is loose
  • bait dropped — Every gull on the wharf has seen it
  • too close — Too near the counter
  • wrong deck — Not from out on the water

All plain American English. No quay in anything a player reads — it is in this document and in the source because it is the right word for a thing, and it is the dock everywhere it has to send somebody.

The ending

Losing headline: The Tide Turns. Winning: you come back standing on a harbor light in the middle of open water with no way down that a person could take, and the gulls are still there, and one of them is looking at you the way it has been looking at you all afternoon — because it was always going to be one of you two, and you got the coin.

§10 Decisions worth vetoing

Ranked by what it costs to reverse, cheapest last.

VETO 1
The setting A fishing wharf, because it is the one ordinary place where broken ground needs no explanation, and because a pier full of gulls is the joke this game did not pick. If this is wrong it is wrong now — the geometry is the expensive part and none of it exists yet.
VETO 2
Relaxing "nothing is built inside the water" to a declared exemption This changes a rule that has been true since level 1, on all five blocks. I think it is a net strengthening because the escape test goes from 24 sampled points to a grid — but it is the change I would most want a second opinion on, and the fallback is a wharf with no pilings, which is worse geometry and no rule change.
VETO 3
$40 Both arguments are in §3. You overruled this number once, on a block that had nowhere else to work; this one has four pitches and $8.50 nobody guards. One line in levels.js either way, and the geometry does not care.
VETO 4
Five decks after the lobby's three It reads like a step back toward the roofline and it is not — the tallest unbroken climb is 3.1 m against the roofline's much longer ones. The decks are close together because they are boats, and what is hard about them is the water between, not the height. If it plays as a climbing block I have built the wrong thing.
VETO 5
dayStart 0.58 Lamps at 2 m 40 s. One rung past the lobby. A knob, and the least consequential thing here.

§11 Questions, and three I am inheriting

Open on this block

  • Whether water reads as a cost or as a punishment. The whole design rests on a swim being eight annoying seconds rather than a lost run. Nothing in a harness can tell me which one it is.
  • Whether a guard on a boat is interesting or just unreachable. The skipper cannot leave his deck, so he guards $9.40 that you have to land next to him for. That is either the best guard in the game or a man shouting at you from a pot.
  • Whether the beacon is far enough out. Too near and the water is decoration; too far and every bank is a chore. This is a number I will move after playing it, and the first number is a guess.

Inherited from the lobby, and what I am doing with them

  • Two gulls on the gallery — interesting or annoying? Answered by taking the other side: the wharf's gulls are everywhere and none of them guards anything. They are the block's weather and its bait mob, and if the lobby's two read as punishing, these are the argument for what gulls are actually for.
  • A 7 m clock on the centre line — landmark or obstacle? Inherited directly. The beacon is the same bet, moved into water where nothing has to walk past it.
  • Eight people in a 44 m room — watched or crowded? Sidestepped. Seven people spread over 60 m, which is the park's density, on a block whose pressure comes from the floor rather than from the cast.

And one bug I found in shipped copy

Level 3's bait toast reads "Chips. Gulls like chips." and its pickup label is A CONE OF CHIPS. That is the British word for fries in copy an American player reads, and it is specifically on the list of things not to do. It is two strings on a shipped block, so it is yours to call — I have not touched it.
