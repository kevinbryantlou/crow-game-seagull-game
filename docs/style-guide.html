Art Direction & Style Guide  ·  v1.0

Late AfternoonMunicipal

Flat-shaded, untextured, low-poly. A public plaza at the hour when the light goes orange and the shadows go violet — and one bird in it that the light refuses to flatter.

§1 The direction

Untitled Goose Game is a soft English garden: pastel, hazy, tidy, no hard edges. That works because a goose in a manicured village is funny by contrast. Our crow is not out of place — a crow on a city block is the most natural thing in the world — so the comedy has to come from somewhere else, and the art has to do a different job.

So: not a garden. A municipal plaza at golden hour. Poured concrete, painted steel, a war memorial nobody reads, a fountain full of other people's wishes. Everything is warm, dusty and slightly civic — and every surface is lit by a low sun that makes cheap concrete look expensive.

The palette is built on one relationship: warm sunlit faces, violet shade. Not grey shade, not black shade. Every shadow in the game leans blue-violet, which makes the golden light read as genuinely golden and gives us a free way to keep the crow legible — he is the one thing that stays dark in the light and darkest in the shade.

One-sentence direction A diorama of a real city block, carved out of coloured paper and lit by a sun that is about to leave.

§3 Light and shading

Flat shading only — flatShading: true on every material, no smooth normals anywhere. Each triangle takes exactly one value, which is what makes a low-poly model read as carved rather than cheap.

The rig

  • One key light. Directional, low, warm #FFD9A0, coming from behind-left of camera at about 22° elevation. It casts the only shadows.
  • Hemisphere fill. Sun haze above, violet bounce below. This is what stops shade going grey — the bounce colour is the shadow colour.
  • No specular. Lambert only. The single exception is the crow's sheen, which is faked with a flat colour on upward-facing faces, not with a shader.
  • Fog matched to sun haze, starting far out, so the block edges dissolve instead of ending.

Three tones, no more

Any object may show a lit face, a mid face and a shade face. Anything that needs a fourth value is over-modelled. This keeps silhouettes readable at phone size, which is the real constraint the whole style is built around.

Exhibit B — the three-tone model. The right-hand cube is the failure case: correct values, no hue in the shade, and the sun stops existing.

§4 The crow

He is a wedge with a hinge in it. Deep chest, short neck, heavy head, and a beak that is unreasonably large because the beak is the cursor — every interaction in the game happens at its tip, so the player's eye needs to find it instantly.

Roughly 220 triangles. Proportions are pushed past real corvid anatomy in exactly two places: the head is about 30% oversized, and the legs are stubbier and further back, which is what produces the indignant forward-tilted walk.

Exhibit C — character sheet. Note that the sheen colour appears only on upward-facing planes; from below the bird is a flat silhouette.

Animation

No skeletons, no imported clips. Every pose is procedural — parts are transformed directly on a small rig of nested groups, which keeps the whole character under a kilobyte and makes it trivially retargetable when the model changes.

  • Walk — legs alternate, body bobs on a sine, head counter-bobs so it stays level. The head staying still while the body moves is the entire read.
  • Flap — wings sweep on a fast attack and slow release. Body pitches nose-up on the downstroke.
  • Glide — wings held out flat, a slow roll into turns.
  • Idle — a head tick every few seconds, at random. This one beat does more for making the bird feel alive than everything else combined.
  • Carry — the item parents to the beak tip and inherits its transform, with a lag spring so it swings.
  • Sit — people only, and only one of them. See §5.

§5 People and props

Humans are built from six boxes and are never given faces. Two reasons: a faceless human is a shape rather than a person, which keeps stealing from them funny instead of mean; and the whole game is read from thirty feet up, where a face would be four pixels of noise. State is communicated by posture and a marker over the head, never by expression.

Sitting, and why exactly one character does it

There is one posture in the game that is not a variation on standing, and it exists for one person: the kid who trades shinies sits down.

It was added because she stopped being findable. On the first block she reads instantly — she is the only human in an empty corner of a plaza. Put the same figure on a restaurant terrace with a maître d', a busser and two diners on it and she is a slightly shorter adult in a crowd of adults, and a playtester could not tell who to hand a bottle cap to. Her colour is already distinct and she is already holding a paper cup; neither survives the distance this camera sits at.

A silhouette does. Nobody else in either block sits, so the shape alone says this one is different before you are close enough to see what she is holding — and a row of trinkets on the ledge beside her says what she does without a marker or a tooltip. The general rule the game took from it: when a character has to be identified at thirty feet, change the shape, not the shade.

The cost is four lines on the existing rig — hips dropped, legs rotated forward, one swinging wider than the other. It stays reserved. A second sitting character would spend the whole value of the first one.

Props follow one rule: a thing you can take is the only thing with a glint on it. There is no outline system and no highlight shader — money and shinies carry a small rotating gold quad, and nothing else in the game does.

Exhibit D — prop and NPC sheet. Human silhouettes change between states so intent is readable at distance without reading the marker.

§6 Camera and composition

38° pitch, 25° yaw, 20° field of view on a long lens sitting far back — near orthographic, so the block reads as a model on a table. The camera never rotates, which means the player builds a stable mental map of the block within about a minute, and every location becomes memorable by its position on screen.

Exhibit E — composition study. Three depth bands. The crow sits on a third-line, and the play plane is the only band that gets full saturation.

The sunset ramp

Over a session the key light rotates about 20°, drops in elevation, and shifts from #FFD9A0 to #FF9E5C to #6E6AA8, with the sky lerping toward Dusk and fog thickening. Shadows get longer and more violet. Nothing else in the game communicates time, and nothing else needs to.

§7 Interface

The HUD borrows the block's own vocabulary — municipal enamel signage, the same language as this document. It is a game about a city, so the interface is made of the things a city puts on a pole.

  • Money counter — top left, enamel green plate, brass rule under it, tabular numerals so the digits never jitter. It is the only element that animates: a coin lands and the number rolls.
  • Task list — top right, small, struck through when done. Never more than five lines, and it carries its own progress count. On touch it introduces itself, folds down to that count after twelve seconds, and reopens on a tap — or by itself, briefly, whenever something is ticked off. On a phone the expanded list is about a third of the upper screen; on a desktop it costs nothing, so it stays open there.
  • Sun dial — a thin arc, bottom right, one dot travelling it. No numbers. Time should be felt, not read.
  • Context prompt — floats at the beak, names the verb and the object, and carries the key that performs it: [J] TAKE — DOLLAR BILL.
  • Nest pointer — a brass chevron that appears only while you are carrying money you have not banked, and retires for good the moment you stash something. It sits over the memorial when the nest is on screen and clamps to the edge of the frame, pointing, when it is not.

Type in the build matches this page: a condensed grotesque in caps for labels, monospace for numbers. No serif in-game — it would not hold at phone size.

Phone layout is its own design Two elements move on touch rather than merely shrinking. The sun dial leaves the bottom-right corner entirely, because that corner belongs to the thumb cluster and nothing else may sit there. The task list stops being permanent furniture: it says its piece, folds to a count, and comes back when asked or when something is ticked off. The rule underneath both — on a small screen, anything not needed right now should be recoverable rather than resident.
On the seventh element This page used to say there were six, and that anything a seventh would need to say could be said by the world instead. The nest pointer is the exception that proves the rule rather than a breach of it: it is the only HUD element with an end condition. It exists because a first-time player is told to take money to their nest without being told where that is — and it removes itself permanently the moment they demonstrate they know. Any future addition should have to clear the same bar: transient, teaching, and self-retiring. Permanent status belongs on the enamel plates.

§8 The link preview card

The first thing most people will see of this game is not the game — it is a 1200×630 rectangle in somebody's messages. It is rendered from the running build rather than composed in a design tool, so it cannot drift from what the game actually looks like, and it is regenerated by a script rather than exported by hand.

The fixed camera cannot zoom, which makes this harder than it sounds: the crow is always small. The framings divide into pretty scenery where the bird is a speck and a legible bird against plainer scenery. The second kind wins. A card for a game about a crow should contain a visible crow, and the chosen frame goes further — an NPC in it has a question mark over his head, so the image shows the game's verb rather than its furniture.

Rules for the card

  • Render it, never mock it up. If the palette changes, the card changes with it on the next run.
  • Wash from the left, never a box. Type sits on a soft gradient over the scene; a solid panel would cut the diorama in half.
  • The wordmark, then one line. Nothing else. No screenshots of UI, no feature list, no button.
  • The strapline and the description are written as a pair. They appear together, so they share a register and share no words — the description is vague and funny, the card supplies the number and the verb.
  • Check the square crop. Some clients centre-crop to a square; the wordmark has to survive it.

§9 Sound

Synthesised in the browser, no audio files — same argument as the geometry. A small Web Audio graph gives us a caw, wingbeats, coin chimes pitched to a pentatonic scale so that a good run is musical, footstep ticks, a water plop, and a distant traffic bed.

The caw is the signature and gets the most work: three formant-ish filtered noise bursts with a pitch drop, slightly randomised each time so it never sounds sampled. Coins rise in pitch as the total climbs toward twenty, which turns the counter into a thing you hear.

§10 Do / don't

Do

  • Keep shade violet, always
  • Three tones per object, maximum
  • Flat shading on every material
  • Let the crow be the darkest thing on screen
  • Communicate state with posture first
  • Put a glint only on what can be taken
  • Silhouette-test everything at 200px

Don't

  • Add textures, normal maps or gradients on geometry
  • Use neutral grey for shadow
  • Give humans faces or dialogue
  • Outline anything — depth does the separating
  • Use pure black or pure white anywhere
  • Let the camera rotate
  • Introduce a colour that is not in §2
