§1 What the darkness measures

Mean luminance of the block — the lower 58% of a 1000×640 frame, HUD hidden, crow parked in the plaza, sampled from decoded screenshots at ten points in the day. Numbers are sRGB 0–255.

Exhibit A · block luminance across the day · measured 2026-08-10

There are two different problems, not one

The mean falls smoothly, which hides the interesting part. The median does not: it goes 113 → 45 between t=0.60 and t=0.70, a 60% collapse in one tenth of the day, while the 95th percentile only drops 135 → 111. Most of the frame falls into shadow while a few strips stay bright.

  • t ≈ 0.70 – 0.88 is a contrast problem. The sun elevation drops from 0.24 to 0.12 and the shadows go long and raking. At t=0.70 the block spans p05 25 to p95 111 — half of it is in deep shade with the other half still bright, and the eye adapts to the bright half. This is the "rust-red rake" in the playtest note.
  • t ≈ 0.88 – 1.00 is an absolute darkness problem. Mean 27 → 20, median 24 → 19. Nothing is bright any more. keyI is 0.9 and amb 0.55 at the end of SKY_RAMP. There is no second source to fall back on because there is no second source at all.

They want different answers. The first wants fill in the shadowed half. The second wants put actual light sources on the block. A plan that only does one of them fixes half the last third of the game.

A target worth arguing about Keep the block's median above ~55 and its 5th percentile above ~35 for the whole session. Both are currently breached from about t=0.68. These are the numbers I would assert in shoot.mjs, and they are guesses until you look at a frame that hits them — that is the first thing to check, not the last.

§2 What the style guide allows

Four of the seven non-negotiables in docs/style-guide.html bear directly on this, and one of them rules out the obvious answer.

RuleWhat it means here
No textures or gradients on geometry A baked lightmap is a gradient texture on geometry. This rule is the single biggest constraint on the whole problem — see §4.
Flat shading, three tones per object Smooth pools of falloff across a face fight the carved look. Light should arrive as separate lit things, not as soft shading on existing things.
Shade is violet, never neutral grey Every lamp is warm amber. Warm light on violet shade is the right contrast, but additive amber over a violet ground desaturates toward grey if pushed. Cap the pools well below full.
The crow is the darkest thing on screen Hard constraint on where light may fall. A crow standing in a pool of lamplight breaks the rule that the player uses to find the crow. See §7.

And one convention from CLAUDE.md that is not about looks: no asset pipeline. Every model is generated from primitives, there is nothing to download at runtime, and the whole build is ~155 kB gzipped. Anything that adds an image file is spending the thing the project is proudest of.

The trap that will bite whoever implements this

shapes.js caches materials by colour. 38 meshes share the single cached goldLit material — the four lamp bulbs and every lit window in the skyline. Setting .emissive on it lights all thirty-eight at once.

That is mostly a gift: the whole city coming on in one assignment is exactly the effect we want, for free. But it means you cannot stagger them, tune them separately, or light one without the others until you clone. Any per-object emissive work starts with mesh.material = mesh.material.clone(), and cloning 38 materials gives back 38 shader programs where there was one. Decide which you want before writing anything.

§4 On pre-baking

The hypothesis on the table was: pre-baked light textures that only appear after 80%, as the lowest-lift and least taxing option. The instinct is right and the implementation is wrong, and the distinction is worth being precise about because the right answer is hiding inside the wrong one.

Right: precompute the look instead of computing the lighting. Nothing about a streetlamp changes frame to frame. Paying a per-fragment lighting cost sixty times a second to redraw the same amber ellipse is waste, and avoiding it is the correct instinct.

Wrong: a lightmap specifically. Three reasons, in order of how hard they are to argue with.

  1. There is nothing to bake onto. The block is generated from primitives at runtime. There is no authored mesh, no UV layout, and no second UV channel. You would have to build all of that first, and then keep it in sync with a level file that is edited by moving numbers around in JavaScript.
  2. It breaks two stated conventions — textures on geometry, and the no-asset-pipeline rule. The ground alone is 120×90 m; a lightmap over it at any usable resolution is a large image in a build that currently downloads nothing.
  3. It is static. The vendor walking out of his own stall light during the pigeon distraction is the best thing that could happen to Cart Corner at dusk, and a lightmap cannot do it.
The same idea, expressed in this codebase Technique B is the pre-baked lightmap, done the way this project already does things. A radial gradient generated once into a 128×128 canvas at boot, reused by every pool, drawn as a flat additive quad on the paving. Precomputed: yes. Zero per-light shader cost: yes. Ships as an asset: no — it is about forty lines that generate their own texture. This is not a new idea in the game either: the pickup glint in world/pickups.js is exactly this technique, already shipped, already generating its gradient from a canvas at runtime.

§6 The plan, in tiers

Each tier is shippable on its own and each ends with a reason to stop. The point of the ordering is that the cheap tiers might be enough, and we will not know until we look at a frame.

TIER 0
Raise the floor Technique E · minutes · zero runtime cost

Add a fifth SKY_RAMP stop around t=0.93 and lift the terminal amb from 0.55. Re-measure. This is the safety net that guarantees the game is playable even if every other tier is cut, and it must land first so the later tiers are tuned against a floor rather than against a hole.

Shipped — and it was more than four numbers A fifth stop at t=0.93, and amb lifted from 0.62/0.55 to 1.05/1.40 across the last two stops. It carried the median from 19 to 53 on its own, which is most of the navigability problem.

It also turned out to be where the violet question lived. fill is now its own column in SKY_RAMP rather than a copy of the horizon glow — see §8, which was written as an aside and turned out to be the load-bearing part of the tier.
TIER 1
Turn everything on Technique A · half a day · zero runtime cost

Ramp emissive on the sources: bulbs and skyline windows together (one shared material, one assignment), café windows, a new bulb under the newsstand canopy, the cart griddle, scaffolding beads, the fountain water.

This does not illuminate anything. It changes the read completely anyway — a dark block with forty warm points in it is a city at night, where a dark block with none is a bug. It is also the cheapest tier by a distance and the one I would ship first if only one shipped.

Shipped Eleven emissive sources in render/nightlights.js: four staggered lampposts, the skyline on one shared material, the café windows and door, a new clip-on lamp over the newsstand counter, the cart griddle, three scaffolding beads, and the fountain. All emissive, no lights added to the scene, no measurable frame cost.

One number needed correcting by eye rather than by arithmetic: the fountain went in at 0.22 of waterLit and read as a floodlit swimming pool that outshone every glint in the plaza — the exact thing §8 says must not happen. It is the largest emissive surface on the block by a wide margin. At 0.055 of the darker water it reads as a lit pool at night.
TIER 2
Pools on the ground Technique B · a day · one draw call per pool

One generated radial-gradient texture, reused. A flat additive quad at y ≈ 0.02 under each lamp, the cart, the newsstand, the café frontage, the kid. Opacity ramps in with the same curve as Tier 1's emissive.

This is the tier that answers "I cannot see where to walk", and it is the honest version of the pre-baked idea from §4. Watch for two things: additive amber over violet paving desaturating toward grey if pushed past about 0.35, and z-fighting with the paving slabs at y = 0.012 — the pools need to sit above them and write no depth.

Shipped — after two rounds of playtest Nine pools, two falloff profiles, one canvas gradient per profile. Both predicted risks turned up and one of them had a cause nobody would have guessed from the code: see §11. The desaturation warning was right, but the mechanism was a missing colorSpace on the texture rather than the opacity being pushed too far.
TIER 3
Three real lights, at most Technique C · a day + measurement · the only tier with a real budget question

Only where a pool provably cannot do the job: the memorial uplight (a vertical shaft), the cart and the newsstand (people who walk in and out of their own light). No shadow casting — the key light stays the only shadow caster, as it is today.

Three PointLights on MeshLambert across 22 materials is very likely fine; the reason it is last is that it is the only option here whose cost has to be measured rather than reasoned about. Measure on the 390×844 @2× mobile pass, not on the desktop one.

Cut if The mobile frame time moves at all. Tier 2 already carried the navigability; this tier is buying charm, and charm does not get to cost frames.

§7 When they come on

The note said 80%. I would argue for t = 0.72, for three reasons.

  1. It is when the problem starts, not when it becomes intolerable. The median breaches the floor at about t=0.68. Light that arrives after the player is already lost reads as a fix; light that arrives just before reads as a world.
  2. Real streetlights come on while there is still daylight. They switch on and look faintly pointless for ten minutes. That is the correct look and it is free.
  3. It is a second clock. Right now the only warning that the day is ending is the sun dial in the corner of the HUD, which is a widget. "The lamps just came on" is the same information delivered by the world, and it is the more frightening version.

The theatre of it

Ramp over ~6 s of game time, but not all together. Stagger the four lamps by 0.4 s each, and give each one a two-frame flicker before it holds — sodium lamps do this and everyone recognises it. A single soft audio.js tick as each one catches, then nothing. This is perhaps twenty lines and it is the most memorable thing in the document.

Note the constraint from §3: the four bulbs share one material with the 34 skyline windows, so a per-lamp stagger means cloning four materials. That is cheap and worth it; leave the skyline on the shared one and bring the whole city up in a single slow ramp behind the staggered foreground.

§8 What must stay dark

The crow

The crow is the darkest thing on screen is how the player finds the crow. That rule survives Tier 1 and Tier 2 untouched — an emissive window does not light anything, and a ground pool is a quad at y=0.02 that the bird stands on top of and occludes. A crow silhouetted in a pool of lamplight is better than a crow on grey paving.

Tier 3 is where it breaks. A real point light at the cart will put amber on the crow's back, and featherSheen is 0x3c3a63 — violet, and reasonably bright. Options if it looks wrong: exclude the crow via a render layer, or accept it on the grounds that a crow lit from below at a hot dog cart at night is a good image. My instinct is the second, but it is a look call and it is yours.

Either way, the contact shadow in Exhibit E is worth doing regardless and belongs in Tier 2: a small dark radial quad under the bird, opacity ramping in as the key light fades. When the key light is at 0.9 intensity the crow stops casting a real shadow, and losing the shadow is most of why the bird gets hard to place in space.

The open plaza

Do not be tempted to fill the gaps between the pools. The pools only read as light because there is dark between them. If the plaza floor comes up uniformly we are back to Tier 0's grey wash with extra steps.

The glints

At t=0.95 the pickup glints are already the brightest things in the frame, and the game briefly becomes a treasure hunt by glint. That is an accident and it is lovely. Whatever we add must stay below the glints in brightness, or we lose it.

Resolved — and it was not unrelated at all The losing ending says "the shadows have gone violet all the way across the plaza." They do not: from t≈0.8 they read rust-red, because setTimeOfDay copies the sky's low colour into the hemisphere fill (this.fill.color.copy(low)) while groundColor stays at PAL.shade and the key goes to 0xff7a48. The rig was wrong, and the mechanism is worth stating: fill.color is the hemisphere light's sky colour, so it lights every up-facing surface the key does not reach — and the paving is nothing but up-facing surfaces. The violet groundColor only ever touched down-facing faces the paving does not have, so the comment "the ground-bounce colour IS the shadow colour" described an intent the code did not implement.

Using the horizon glow was also wrong physically: flat ground in shadow at sunset is lit by the sky dome overhead, not by the orange band at the horizon. fill is now its own ramp column, warm at midday and swinging violet as the sun drops. Shipped, and asserted both in smoke.mjs (blue beats red in the dusk fill colour) and in shoot.mjs (blue beats red in the actual rendered frame). The losing ending's line about violet shadows is now true.

§9 How we will know it worked

Per the repo's first convention, none of this counts until it has been looked at, and preferably until it has been asserted. Three additions:

  1. Dusk shots in shoot.mjs. Six frames at t = 0.6, 0.72, 0.8, 0.88, 0.95, 1.0 from a fixed crow position. They go in shots/ and they get read.
  2. A luminance floor, asserted. The screenshot-decode used for Exhibit A is about twenty lines and runs in the page. Fail the build if the block's median drops below 55 or its 5th percentile below 35 at any sampled t. This is the check that would have caught the current state before a playtest did.
  3. A frame-time budget on the mobile pass. Only needed if Tier 3 happens, and it is the gate for whether Tier 3 ships at all.

One caveat on the numbers in Exhibit A: they were measured under SwiftShader, the software renderer the headless harness uses. Absolute values on real hardware will differ a little. The shape of the curve and the relative collapse of the median will not, and that is what the argument rests on.

§10 What I would want decided

  1. Violet or rust? Settled: violet. Three reasons rather than one — the style guide says so, the physics says so, and warm lamps need cool shade or the whole night is a single hue. See §8.
  2. How much throw, and where? Settled: pools everywhere. Shipped after two rounds — see §11. Whether the two stalls still want a real light on top is the remaining tier 3 question.
  3. Does the crow get lit at the cart? A rule against a good image. Only matters if tier 3 happens — pools sidestep it entirely, because the bird occludes the quad it is standing on. See §11.
  4. Tier 3 — parked, and it is the only expensive one. Worth being precise about why, because the first three tiers were cheap for structural reasons rather than by luck. Tier 0 is four numbers. Tier 1 is emissive, a constant term in a fragment shader that already runs. Tier 2 is nine unlit quads — nine draw calls and a little overdraw. A PointLight is different in kind: it recompiles all thirty lit materials with NUM_POINT_LIGHTS=N and then pays per-fragment lighting for N lights on every lit pixel of every frame, near a lamp or not. It is the only option whose cost scales with resolution, which is why it bites hardest on a phone.

    Three shadowless point lights on Lambert is probably fine, and "probably" is doing real work in that sentence. It cannot be settled from the harness: shoot.mjs runs SwiftShader, a software renderer, so its frame times say nothing about a real GPU. The measurement is about ten minutes — add the lights at runtime from the console on a real phone, watch frame time, decide. No code change needed to get the number.
  5. Tier 2.5 — the cheap version of tier 3, untested. What tier 3 actually buys is people lit at their stalls: the newsagent behind his counter, and the vendor walking out of his own light during the pigeon distraction, which is the best thing that could happen to Cart Corner after dark. That might not need real lights at all. Warm-tinting a human's material while they stand inside a stall's radius is a per-entity tweak on six characters rather than per-fragment work on every pixel, and it sidesteps the crow-lighting question in §8 entirely.

    Cruder, and at this camera distance possibly indistinguishable. Worth trying before tier 3, because if it works the expensive tier never has to happen. It inherits the same trap as everything else here — shapes.mat() caches by colour, so the humans would need cloned materials first.
  6. How far do we take the night? Deferred until Tiers 0 and 1 have been played. There is a version of this where t=0.95 is genuinely dark and the game becomes a different game for ninety seconds — glints, pools, and a bird you track by its contact shadow. It is a better ending than "it got hard to see". It is also a bigger change than the note asked for, and it makes the last stretch harder rather than easier.
  7. Is 18 minutes still right? No — it is eight now. Lighting the back third was not the whole answer after all: at eighteen minutes the lamps caught at 12m58s against a 2m07s competent run, so all of tiers 0–2 was unreachable in normal play. Shortening the day was what made the work visible. The failure is now asserted in both directions — a fast run must still finish in daylight, and the lamps must not be stranded past a normal run.

§11 Playtest, 11 August

Tiers 0 and 1 played. The catch reads, the violet shade reads — "does make for a better contrast in the evening, the user is able to see better in the dark" — and the fountain sits at the right level. One note, and it is the one the brief predicted:

The finding "The clip-on lamp over the newsstand just looks like a light blip — with no light throw it just looks like a dot."

This is tier 1 working exactly as specified rather than a defect. §6 promised "This does not illuminate anything. It changes the read completely anyway." It does for the four lampposts, which are tall and read against sky. It does not for a small fixture low down and surrounded by geometry that stays dark — there, a bright pixel with no consequence reads as a sticker.

Light throw is tier 2. It was prototyped, photographed, reviewed, corrected once, and is now shipped. The pairs below are the prototype as first built; the pairs further down are what it took to make it right.

A lamppost on open paving

The clearest case. Before, a gold bead 4.6 m up and nothing beneath it. After, the crow is a silhouette standing at the edge of its own pool — which is the image the style guide wants, not a compromise with it.

The newsstand — the reported symptom

Cart Corner

Griddle, parasol and the scaffolding beads. The pool is offset to the serving side, so the vendor has somewhere lit to stand and somewhere dark to be lured away from.

Round two: the pools were washed out, and the stalls looked cheap

Shipping the prototype for review produced two notes. "The lighting pools around the lampposts look just okay, but the point lighting looks a little cheap on the vendor stands — I'm thinking it should be a more even pool of light. This may also just be a function of the lavender-white colour vs sodium amber." Both halves were right, and both were measurable.

The colour. Sampled three metres from a lamppost, R−B was +2 against +3 on bare paving, with saturation falling from 32% to 26%. The pool was 40% brighter and not one bit warmer — adding neutral light and washing the violet out. §2 predicted the symptom and blamed the opacity. The opacity was innocent.

The actual cause A CanvasTexture defaults to NoColorSpace. three.js therefore read the gradient as linear data while the renderer output sRGB, so an amber authored as (255,206,120) reached the screen as roughly (255,234,183) — a near-white cream. No amount of retinting the stops could have fixed it. One line — tex.colorSpace = THREE.SRGBColorSpace — took the newsstand from R−B +8 to +22 at identical luminance.

world/pickups.js has the same omission on the glint texture. Invisible there, because the glint is near-white by design — but it is the same latent bug, and worth fixing whenever that file is next open.

The shape. A separate mistake with the same smell: every pool used one falloff. A bulb 4.6 m up a pole is a point source and wants a hot core; a bulb under a stall canopy is an area source a metre wide, and giving it the lamppost's spike is precisely what made the stands read as a bright dot with a halo rather than a lit counter. There are two profiles now — lamp keeps the core, stall is a plateau, near-flat to 45% of its radius then a soft shoulder — and the stall pools widened from 3.2 m to 4.4 and 3.8 to 4.8.

What it cost

Nine pools: four lampposts, the cart, the newsstand, three across the café frontage. One 128×128 radial gradient generated into a canvas at boot and shared by every one of them — no asset, no second UV set, no per-light shader work, and no lights added to the scene. Each pool is one additive quad at y=0.05 with depthWrite: false, riding the same catch-and-warm clock as the bulb above it, so a pool flickers on with its lamp.

It also keeps the rule that mattered most. The pool is a quad on the ground that the crow stands on top of and occludes, so the bird is silhouetted in the light rather than lit by it — the plaza image above. A real point light in the same place would put amber on its back.

Both of the things that blocked it are done. The night-light assertions now read whichever output a light actually drives, and there are two new ones worth having: pool textures must be tagged sRGB, and pools must be additive with depthWrite: false — the property that keeps the crow a silhouette rather than something the light falls on.

Still open: tier 3

Pools do not light people. The two places the brief flagged for a real light are exactly the two stalls — a newsagent behind a counter and a vendor who walks out of his own light during the pigeon distraction are both vertical, and both are people. Pools first and a scoped tier 3 after remains the recommendation, and the pools may well be enough on their own — that is now a question to answer by playing rather than by arguing.

§12 The pools were drawn all day

Found on 2026-08-14, chasing a reported frame-rate dip at dusk. The dip was not at dusk: there is no dusk-specific rendering step at all — no shader recompiles, no new draw calls, no time-of-day branch in the frame loop, and day-to-night frame times that differ by noise. The lights coming on cost essentially nothing. What the measurement turned up instead was worse.

A pool is created transparent: true at opacity: 0, and three.js draws transparent materials regardless of opacity. So every pool on every block was rasterised and blended across its whole disc from the moment the level loaded — the entire daylight half of a run spent on fill that adds nothing to any pixel.

Two and a half to four screens of additive, depth-write-off overdraw, for something invisible. Pools now carry a mesh reference and are visible = false until their level crosses zero.

The counts are the argument, not the milliseconds The first version of this section quoted “16% of the lobby’s midday frame”. That figure came from software rasterisation on a machine two sessions were hammering, and a later measurement on the same setup produced a 4× spread across identical scenes with identical draw counts. Wall-clock timing here is not trustworthy for small deltas. What is trustworthy is a count: 18 quads at 319% coverage drawn for something nobody can see is a defect at any millisecond price, and the fix is one line.
The check that guarded it passed for free “No pool is drawn in daylight” was first asserted after the block’s ramp had been driven up and wound back — and winding back runs update’s own toggle, which sets visible = false by itself. The assertion was reading a value update had written, so deleting the line in addPool that it exists to guard left it green with the overdraw back. It now runs before any update touches a fresh block, and it was tested against the broken value first: with the line removed it fails on all five blocks, 9/9, 11/11, 12/12, 18/18 and 14/14 drawn at build.
